# Chapter 10 Recordings, Performances, and Cross-Genre Collaborations

## 10.1 Collaboration with the Academy of St Martin in the Fields

Tommy Reilly's collaboration with the Academy of St Martin-in-the-Fields was one of the most brilliant chapters in his artistic career. Founded by Neville Marriner in 1958, this orchestra is renowned for its clear texture, precise rhythm, and elegant style, becoming the most influential British chamber orchestra outside the "early music movement" after World War II. Reilly's ability to become its regular solo partner indicates that his playing style was highly compatible with the orchestra's aesthetic philosophy.

![img_080.jpeg](assets/img_080.jpeg)

He recorded two albums with orchestras, including several harmonica concertos and solo pieces, which are considered milestones in harmonica music.

Conductor Neville Marriner remarked: "Many of the early aspirations of the Academy Orchestra are reflected in Tommy's musical talent: technically, he achieved outstanding virtuosity with the least fuss. Musically, he developed his instrument in an exquisite and flamboyant way, ultimately making it seem that what he played was not important, but rather how he played it."

## 10.2 Important Recordings and Performance Memories

Reilly's important recordings include "Tommy Reilly Plays Harmonica Concertos and Virtuoso Works" (1993, Chandos Records), featuring works by Spivakovsky, Farnon, and Jacob; "Colours of my Life" (1968) with Sigmund Groven; and numerous recordings with the BBC, Norwegian Broadcasting, German Broadcasting, Dutch Broadcasting, and more.

### Unforgettable Performance Experience
**1951 Debut at the Festival of Britain**: The premiere of Spivakovsky's *Harmonica Concerto* at the BBC Broadcasting House Concert Hall in London marked the harmonica's official entry into the realm of classical music.

1953 Stuttgart "Light Music Week": Achieved great success, immediately leading to invitations from radio and television stations across Europe.

1953 Oslo recording of *Zigeunerweisen*: Includes live audience applause, capturing the direct atmosphere of a historical performance.

### 1966 Performance Before the King of Norway

Performance of Farnon's *Prelude and Dance* premiering in Oslo, with King Olav V of Norway in attendance.

Music festivals around the world: Participated in the UK's Proms, the Netherlands' Holland Festival, Germany's Bayreuth Festival, and more.

## 10.3 Breakthrough in Film Scoring

Reilly's most celebrated contribution to film scores was his harmonica solo on the theme for the 1969 film *Midnight Cowboy*. Composed by John Barry, this Oscar-winning Best Picture film featured a harmonica solo that became a classic moment in film music history. The harmonica's lonely, melancholic tone perfectly matched the film's narrative of life on the margins in New York City, creating a powerful emotional resonance. Reilly's playing imbued the theme with deep lyricism and memorability, setting a benchmark for the use of the harmonica in serious film music.

Many people are unaware of this part of the story, so they often get it wrong. In short, all the soundtrack recordings or film clip soundtracks you see and hear online are played by Reilly. To hear Thielemans play this piece, you can only watch the film or listen to his live performances.

The 1969 Oscar-winning film *Midnight Cowboy* is known for its theme song played by Toots Thielemans, but when the film soundtrack was later released, it was Tommy Reilly who played the harmonica parts.

Toots Thielemans was a Belgian jazz harmonica master, with his rendition appearing in the actual film, while Reilly's version was featured on the official soundtrack album. The exact reason for this substitution remains unclear, but it was likely due to the commercial decisions or musical style preferences of the record company at the time. Reilly's version is more classical, while Thielemans' version has a distinct jazz style.

![img_081.png](assets/img_081.png)

In addition to *Midnight Cowboy*, Reilly also contributed to the soundtracks of several other significant films, including *The Sundowners* (1960), among others. A common feature of these works is the full utilization of the harmonica's narrative potential - its ability to express both folk, rustic emotions and modern, urban loneliness. Reilly's technical precision and musical expressiveness made him the preferred harmonica soloist for film composers.

![img_082.png](assets/img_082.png)

Reilly's network of conductor collaborations spanned multiple genres including classical, light music, and film scores. In the classical realm, he recorded several concertos with conductors such as Neville Marriner, Charles Gerhardt, and Cedric Dumont; in film music, he worked with renowned composer-conductors like Bernard Herrmann, Elmer Bernstein, Dimitri Tiomkin, Maurice Jarre, Jerry Goldsmith, and John Barry, performing harmonica solos for numerous film scores.

### From *The Wanderer* to *55 Days at Peking*: Other Films and Television Themes

### Breakthrough in Film Scoring

In the late 1940s, Tommy Reilly began to make a name for himself in the world of film scoring. His unique style and technical mastery of the chromatic harmonica caught the attention of many composers and directors. One of his earliest notable contributions was to the 1949 film *"The Blue Lagoon"*, where his harmonica playing added a distinctive emotional depth to the soundtrack.

![Tommy Reilly](https://example.com/tommyreilly.jpg)

Reilly's work on film scores continued to grow, and he collaborated with several renowned composers. His performance on the theme for the 1952 film *"The Snows of Kilimanjaro"* is particularly celebrated. This piece, composed by Bernard Herrmann, showcased Reilly's ability to convey complex emotions through his instrument.

| Year | Film Title          | Composer          |
|------|---------------------|-------------------|
| 1949 | *The Blue Lagoon*  | Miklós Rózsa      |
| 1952 | *The Snows of Kilimanjaro* | Bernard Herrmann |
| 1955 | *The Man from Laramie* | George Duning    |

Reilly's influence extended beyond his own performances. He inspired other harmonica players, such as Sigmund Groven and Uwe Warschkow, to explore the possibilities of the instrument in film music. His legacy in this field is evident in the continued use of the harmonica in film scores today.

> "Tommy Reilly's contributions to film music are immeasurable. He brought a new level of artistry to the harmonica and opened doors for future generations." - Sigmund Groven

### Chronology

- **1949**: *The Blue Lagoon* soundtrack released, featuring Reilly's harmonica.
- **1952**: *The Snows of Kilimanjaro* theme recorded.
- **1955**: *The Man from Laramie* features Reilly's playing.

Reilly's work in film scoring remains a testament to his versatility and innovation as a musician.

### From *The Vagabond* to *55 Days at Peking*: The Chromatic Harmonica as the Preferred Choice of Hollywood's Scoring Maestros

In 1960, Reilly recorded the theme music for the film *The Sundowners*, directed by Fred Zinnemann - Dimitri Tiomkin's *Theme from "The Sundowners"* and *Down Under*, accompanied by Wally Stott's orchestra (Philips PN 1094 single; reissued in 1961 as the *Harmonica Magic* EP, featuring tracks like *Yokohama Holiday*). The film's depiction of an Australian outback grazing family naturally aligned with the harmonica's "folk, rustic sentiment."

In 1963, he recorded the two soundtrack pieces *So Little Time* and *Moon Fire* for the epic film *55 Days at Peking* directed by Nicholas Ray (music by Tiomkin, Philips PB 1248, in collaboration with the Ivor Raymonde Choir and Orchestra). In the same year, he also recorded for German Western films (*Dakota*, *S.O.S.*, Oriole CB 1833, with The Tradesmen as accompaniment).

In the 1970s, his film score work extended to Scandinavia: in 1976, the Norwegian animated feature *Flåklypa Grand Prix* (also known as *Pinchcliffe Grand Prix*) had music by Bent Fabricius-Bjerre, with all harmonica parts played by Reilly (Polydor 2382 066 soundtrack album); in 1979, the British film *Tarka the Otter* featured music by David Fanshawe, with Reilly performing alongside an instrumental ensemble (Argo ZSW 613).

In 1981, he recorded the album *Winnetou Melodien* (Teldec 6.24965 AS) for the famous German Winnetou western film series: *Old Shatterhand-Melodie*, *Apanatschi*, *Winnetou Melodie*, *Grand Canyon-Melodie*, and *Chinla-River-Song*, all accompanied by the Munich Radio Orchestra conducted by Martin Böttcher himself – Böttcher being the composer of the film scores for the Winnetou series.

In 1994, the posthumous score of Bernard Herrmann's *The Night Digger* was reissued on CD by X label (LXCD 12), featuring Reilly's harmonica playing in *Scenario Macabre for Orchestra*, conducted by Herrmann himself with the Sessions of London Orchestra. In the same year, he also made a guest appearance on harmonica in Canadian composer André Gagnon's album *Romantique* (Star STR-CD-8057), playing in the track *Le pianiste envolé*, conducted by John Coleman with the National Philharmonic Orchestra.

### The Golden Age of British Television: Harmonica Themes

Reilly left behind many memorable theme songs for British TV and radio comedies:

- **1953** Theme from *The Night of the Fourth* (Parlophone R 3693, conducted by Eric Spear) — Note: This piece was not composed by Reilly, but by May, and is often mistakenly attributed to Reilly.

- **1954** Theme song for the BBC family drama *The Grove Family*, "Family Joke" (composed by Eric Spear, Parlophone R 3860). This piece was later widely used as production music.

- **1958** Theme song for the ITV police drama *Dixon of Dock Green*, "An Ordinary Copper" (Oriole CB 1426, featuring actor Jack Warner). This is one of the most famous harmonica themes in British television history.

- **1960** Theme music for the BBC radio comedy *The Navy Lark* (Hohner T 4007). Reilly co-created this melody with James Moody, which later became a classic; the track *Trade Wind Hornpipe* from the music album is an instrumental arrangement of the theme music. In 1996, EMI Premier's album *Vintage Themes from British Radio, Television & the Newsreels* included the original recordings of these two themes.

### Building a Music Library: The Vast Repertoire Behind the Pseudonym

In addition to commercial recordings, Reilly also recorded hundreds of tracks for production music libraries—these short, functional pieces of music were used as background music in films, television, and radio. He collaborated with James Moody and his son David Reilly, using various pseudonyms—**Max Martin, T Rundle, Dave Holland, Dwight Barker**—to record a vast amount of music for companies such as Conroy, Berry Music, Chappell, Weinberger (JW Media Music), AudioBMP, and Boosey and Hawkes.

The Conroy company's music album *Harmonica* (KCR0160, 29 minutes, 49 tracks) is a collection of works co-owned 50% by Reilly and Moody, featuring tracks such as *Sophisticated Party*, *Wind In Her Hair*, *Trade Wind Hornpipe*, *Child's Play*, *Hurry Hurry*, *Pins And Needles*, *Night Out*, *Shooting The Rapids*, *Alley Cat*, *Mirabelle*, and more—each only lasting from a few dozen seconds to a minute, representing the pinnacle of "functional music."

It is noteworthy that Reilly's recordings credited as "T. Rundle" or "Rundle-Morris" (shared with Moody) (such as *Esmeralda*, *Tokyo*, *No Time*) are an extension of this pseudonym system - "Rundle" being derived from his real name Thomas Rundle Reilly.

## 10.4 Film and Television Soundtrack Chronology

Reilly's harmonica can be heard in numerous film, television, and radio scores. The following chronology is based on the Groven record catalog:

• 1953 Recording of the theme song for the film *The Night of the Fourth* (Parlophone R 3693, conducted by Eric Spear);

• 1954 BBC series *The Grove Family* theme song *Family Joke* (Eric Spear, Parlophone R 3860);

• 1958 Appearance on the theme song "An Ordinary Copper" for the TV series *Dixon of Dock Green* (Oriole CB 1426, featuring actor Jack Warner);

• 1960 Film "The Sundowners" (Tiomkin score, Philips PN 1094 single); BBC radio comedy "The Navy Lark" theme song (Hohner T 4007, co-created with Moody);

• 1963 Film "55 Days at Peking" (Tiomkin soundtrack) featured songs "So Little Time" and "Moon Fire" (Philips PB 1248);

• 1969 Single version of the theme from the film "Midnight Cowboy" (Polydor NH 59323, John Scott Orchestra);

• 1976 Norwegian animated film *Flåklypa Grand Prix* full harmonica score (composed by Bent Fabricius-Bjerre);

• 1979 Film score for *Tarka the Otter* (composed by David Fanshawe, Argo ZSW 613);

• 1981 West German film series *Winnetou* music album (Teldec, Böttcher conducting the Munich Radio Orchestra);

• 1994 Reissue of Bernard Herrmann's posthumous score for *The Night Digger* (Label LXCD 12, Herrmann conducting); harmonica cameo on André Gagnon's album *Romantique* (Star STR-CD-8057);

• 1996 EMI Premier's "Vintage Themes from British Radio, Television & the Newsreels" includes "The Navy Lark" (Moody-Reilly) and "Family Joke" (Spear).

In addition, he recorded numerous unreleased broadcast and film score segments for composers such as Bernard Herrmann, Elmer Bernstein, Dimitri Tiomkin, Maurice Jarre, Jerry Goldsmith, and John Barry.
