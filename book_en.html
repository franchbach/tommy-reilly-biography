Preface

In the classical music scene of the 20th century, there was an instrument that was long overlooked, misunderstood, and even regarded as a "toy" - the harmonica. However, it was on this seemingly unremarkable instrument that a Canadian-born British musician created a legend, elevating the harmonica from a street performer's accompaniment to a solo instrument in symphonic music, earning unprecedented respect and recognition for the instrument. He was Tommy Reilly (1919-2000).

Tommy Reilly's name, for classical music aficionados, represents the pinnacle of harmonica artistry; for harmonica players, he is the undisputed master and pioneer. Over his sixty-year career, Reilly not only captivated audiences worldwide with his virtuosity but also laid a solid foundation for the harmonica in classical music through commissioned works, instrument innovations, and teaching legacy. Over thirty contemporary renowned composers created pieces for him, including Gordon Jacob, Robert Farnon, James Moody, George Martin, and others, a feat unparalleled in harmonica history.

The harmonica, as a portable free-reed instrument, has been systematically excluded from the orthodox halls of classical music since its invention in the early 19th century. Its origins in popular culture, relatively low manufacturing cost, and widespread use in folk and pop music have led to its systematic neglect in the realm of serious music. In the first half of the 20th century, although the harmonica found a place in the North American blues tradition and European light music, the classical music establishment generally regarded it as a "toy instrument" or "street performer's tool," refusing to acknowledge its potential as a solo instrument. This bias was not only evident in the absence of the harmonica in orchestral arrangements but also reflected in the curriculum of music academies, composers' willingness to write for it, and the evaluation system of music critics. Harmonica players were often confined to specific musical style frameworks, finding it difficult to break genre barriers and gain broader artistic recognition.

Tommy Reilly: A Pioneer in Classical Harmonica

Tommy Reilly (1919-2000) was a legendary chromatic harmonica player who challenged these prejudices and carved out a unique niche for the harmonica in the world of classical music. Born in Canada and later based in the UK, Reilly's career spanned over six decades, during which he worked tirelessly to elevate the harmonica's status.

Early Life and Career

Reilly began playing the harmonica at a young age and quickly demonstrated exceptional talent. His professional career took off in the 1940s when he started performing on BBC radio. His virtuosic playing and innovative approach to the instrument caught the attention of composers and audiences alike.

Collaborations and Compositions

Reilly collaborated with numerous composers, including Sigmund Groven and Uwe Warschkow, who wrote original pieces for him. Some of the notable works include:

  • Concerto for Harmonica and Orchestra by Malcolm Arnold
  • Fantasy for Harmonica and Orchestra by Gordon Jacob
  • Suite for Harmonica and Orchestra by Arthur Benjamin

Legacy and Influence

Reilly's recordings and performances helped to dispel the notion of the harmonica as merely a folk or pop instrument. He demonstrated its versatility and expressive range, inspiring future generations of harmonica players. His legacy is evident in the continued interest in classical harmonica repertoire and the growing number of compositions written for the instrument.

"Tommy Reilly's contributions to the harmonica world are immeasurable. He was a true pioneer who opened doors for the instrument in the classical realm." - Harmonica World Magazine

Reilly's influence extends beyond his recordings and performances. He was a dedicated teacher, mentoring many aspiring harmonica players and sharing his passion for the instrument. His work laid the foundation for the harmonica's acceptance in classical music, and his legacy continues to inspire musicians around the world.

This historical context forms an important backdrop for understanding Tommy Reilly's achievements — his artistic career was essentially a decades-long campaign for "legitimacy," aiming to prove that the harmonica was fully capable of the same expressiveness and artistic depth as string and woodwind instruments. As British composer Gordon Jacob aptly put it, "He elevated the harmonica to a solo instrument of high artistic value," a statement that succinctly sums up Reilly's historical contribution.

In his 2019 CD review, Singaporean harmonica player Cheng Jang Ming explicitly stated: "Tommy Reilly is widely recognized as a chromatic harmonica master," a sentiment that reflects the consensus within the international harmonica community. More authoritatively, Igor Stravinsky once personally remarked: "After hearing your interpretation of my Russian Songs, I would be delighted for you to perform any of my works."

Sir Neville Marriner commented on BBC Radio: "Technically, he achieved extraordinary skill with the minimum of fuss. Musically, he developed his instrument in an exquisite and sumptuous way, so that in the end it seemed not to matter what he played, but how he played it." These endorsements from top figures in classical music established Reilly's artistic status beyond the harmonica community. In 1992, he was awarded the Member of the Order of the British Empire (MBE), becoming the first harmonica player to receive this honour.

This book, based on the original biography, specially integrates Tommy Reilly's teaching essence, details of instrument innovations, and precious dialogue records with his students. It also provides an in-depth analysis of his representative works Serenade and Valsentino and their performance techniques. By consulting a large amount of first-hand material, including the commemorative collection Harmonica Lessons with Tommy Reilly written by his student Uwe Warschkow, his two important textbooks The Tommy Reilly Harmonica Course and Studies For Chromatic Harmonica, as well as detailed technical analysis, this book strives to present a true, three-dimensional, and in-depth image of Tommy Reilly - not only as a master performer but also as a rigorous educator, innovative instrument reformer, and loving mentor.

Appendix E Tommy Reilly on Performance: A Collection of Quotes

Note: The following quotes are translated from the course transcript of Uwe Warschkow's "Harmonica Lessons with Tommy Reilly," BBC interviews, and Reilly's own accounts, all rendered into English. They are categorized into six themes, providing the most concise path to understanding Reilly's playing philosophy.

E.1 The Nature of Music

  1. "Music is not notes, music is emotion."

  2. "No musician thinks note by note. Every musician thinks in phrases."

  3. "A musical phrase is the smallest unit of music. Therefore, try to play an entire phrase in one fluid motion."

  4. "They are the questions in the music, with the subsequent notes providing the answers."

  5. "Etudes should also be played musically."

  6. "If you practice the different parts of a piece with the utmost precision, you will gain a sense of the inherent tension within it, and then even if the score doesn't indicate it, you will naturally play it with dynamic variation."

E.2 Harmonica Technical Philosophy

  1. "The principle is: always take the shortest path, make the smallest possible movements."

  2. "The head should always remain stable, only the harmonica needs to move."

  3. "The tongue guides the instrument."

  4. "When performing, you must never let the audience hear whether the note you are playing is blown or drawn."

  5. "I think like a violinist, and I also phrase like a violinist."

  6. "The slide should be pressed down fully and decisively."

  7. "The dynamic range of the harmonica is not as wide as that of other wind instruments. Therefore, you must always pay special attention and consciously utilize the full dynamic range of the harmonica."

  8. "Any tension will affect your playing."

E.3 Legato and Phrasing

  1. "When playing Lullaby, only when the performer can play legato, can they move the audience."

  2. "Every note matters! You must sustain each note for its full duration."

  3. "The ultimate effect should be: no one can tell when you are blowing or drawing. This is the most difficult challenge in harmonica playing. Once you master it, other musicians will accept you as an equal peer."

  4. "Natural breathing points occur before you start a new musical phrase, giving shape to the phrase."

  5. "If you take a breath in the middle of a musical phrase, it will most likely disrupt the arc of the music."

  6. "Hum this phrase to find the best way to express its tension and how to play it on the harmonica."

E.4 Methodology of Practice

  1. "Never practice without concentration! It is not only a waste of time but also dangerous, as you may make such inaccuracies permanent."

  2. "You should never practice like this: first practice bars 1 to 10, then bars 11 to 20, and so on. That is purely mechanical practice, devoid of any musical meaning."

  3. "First, you need to identify the musical phrases: where it begins, where it climaxes, and where it ends."

  4. "Listening to your own playing is the most important part of developing good playing technique."

  5. "A good etude focuses on only one specific aspect of performance technique."

  6. "Etudes are not an end in themselves; I suggest they should be matched with the pieces you are currently learning."

  7. "When learning a new piece, don't just study the harmonica part. The entire work is important."

  8. "A pianist would never make a big arc with their hand just to reach the next octave note."

E.5 Teaching and Listening

  1. "You're trying too hard, stay relaxed. Play it again."

  2. "Correct movements are the foundation for developing good playing technique."

  3. "We cannot overemphasize the importance of refining your movements."

  4. "Never start without being mentally prepared."

  5. "Always be mindful of maintaining the tempo. Amateur players often unconsciously play the louder passages faster and the softer passages slower."

  6. "The contrast between strong and weak must be clearly audible."

  7. "I don't want to be nitpicky, but I do want to refine your movements even more."

E.6 Life and Attitude

  1. "Those days in the prisoner-of-war camp are a part of my life, not five and a half years taken out of my life."

  2. "Immersing myself in studying the harmonica also psychologically helped me to survive."

  3. "I love this land. After returning from tours or escaping the hustle and bustle of the recording studios in London, I can find peace and relax here."

  4. "What infuriates me is that Hohner changed the slide button without consulting me — such a decision could only come from someone who has never played the harmonica."

  5. "For me, silver is the best material because it has proven its worth on the flute."

  6. "The final push was when Robert Farnon composed Prelude and Dance for me. The piece was so technically and musically demanding that I concluded I couldn't meet these requirements with the standard Hohner 270."

  7. "First and foremost, you are a musician, and only then a harmonica player."

Appendix: Points of Historical Uncertainty, Version Differences, and Items for Further Verification

This chapter serves as a supplementary appendix. It compiles historical uncertainties, version discrepancies, and items requiring further verification that were discovered during the writing and verification process of this book, for the reference of readers and researchers. Annotation rules: 【Verified】 = directly supported by primary sources; 【To be verified】 = currently lacking direct evidence.

G.1 Date of Arrest: August or September 1939?

  • Manuscript text (2.1): On September 3, 1939, Reilly was arrested by the Gestapo at a hotel.

  • 【Verified】Uwe quoted Tommy Reilly's own recollection: "I was studying the violin at the famous Leipzig Conservatory... when I was arrested by the Gestapo as an 'enemy alien'. This was in August 1939, just before the outbreak of the Second World War."

  • Analysis: August 1939 was during the escalation of the Polish crisis and on the eve of Germany's full mobilization; it was completely in line with historical facts for the German authorities to arrest enemy aliens on a large scale; after the outbreak of war on September 1st, the arrests became more systematic. Reilly himself clearly remembered it as August in his later years' recollections.

  • Suggestion: Change the main text to "In August 1939, on the eve of the war", and note "Some say September 3rd".

G.2 Harmonica in Prisoner-of-War Camp: A Dozen or Two?

  • Manuscript 2.2 Draft: "In exchange, he got a dozen new Hohner harmonicas."

  • [Verified] Reilly's own words: "the bloke got me two dozens harmonicas from Hohner"; Guardian obituary: "a package of two dozen chromatic harmonicas from Hohner". The revised version has been changed to "two dozen (24)".

  • Additional details: the amount of coffee was "half a pound" (approximately 227 grams, though some say 250 grams); it was exchanged with a "German-Canadian welfare officer," not a "quartermaster."

G.3 Was he arrested with his violin?

  • Manuscript 2.4 Paraphrase David Reilly: "The violin was not confiscated by the Germans... After the war, the violin was never recovered, suspected to have been stolen by British soldiers."

  • [Verified] Reilly's own account: "I also had my violin with me and we had our own orchestra there."——He did have and play the violin during his time in the prisoner-of-war camp, consistent with the manuscript's narrative; the detail about "not receiving the violin after the war" lacks firsthand evidence [to be verified], retained as David Reilly's oral account.

G.7 "The Only Harmonica Player to Receive an MBE" Statement

  • The manuscript frequently uses the phrase "the first and only harmonica player to receive this honor."

  • Analysis: The fact of being appointed MBE in 1992 [verified] (Uwe's book p158-161 records the New Year Honours list announced by the Queen on December 31, 1991). However, "only" is a judgment as of 2000; it is recommended to phrase it as "the first harmonica player to receive this honor, and remained the only one at the time of his passing" for more accuracy.

G.8 Chromatic Harmonica Timeline

  • In 1967, Reilly commissioned a London silversmith to create a sterling silver prototype (with a three-octave range); Douglas Tate assisted with the assembly and tuning; Hohner was initially hesitant but changed their stance upon learning that Yamaha was interested in purchasing the license, leading to a production contract; the harmonica was launched around 1968-1969; Groven received his second one in October 1969. The manuscript's section 6.3 "Officially released in 1967" is incorrect, and the revised edition has been updated to "Entered production in 1968-1969".

G.9 The Mystery of the Two Versions of Midnight Cowboy

  • 【Verified】Harmonica in the movie = Toots Thielemans; Official soundtrack album = Tommy Reilly (1969 London re-recording, Polydor NH 59323 single). The reason for the replacement was never officially explained; according to Groven's recollection, Polydor Hamburg once called Reilly to record the "movie theme", with the producer requesting it to be "exactly like the original recording" -- what they played for him was Reilly's own recording. The 1969 Grammy nomination for Best Contemporary Instrumental Performance went to Ferrante & Teicher's piano version, which John Barry found deeply disappointing.

Chapter 1 Early Years: From Canada to the UK (1919-1939)

1.1 Birth and Childhood

Tommy Reilly was born on August 21, 1919, in Guelph, Ontario, Canada. This is a small city located about 100 kilometers southwest of Toronto, known for its agriculture and manufacturing. Reilly was born into a family with a deep musical background; his father, Captain James Reilly, was a musically talented military band conductor who had served as a bandmaster at the Royal Military School of Music, RMSM Kneller Hall in the UK, before moving to Canada to become the bandmaster at the Guelph garrison, while also leading the OAC Symphony Orchestra and the local choir.

This military music family background provided a unique environment for Tommy's early music education: the technical precision, ensemble discipline, and wide repertoire emphasized in military music traditions profoundly influenced Tommy's future artistic development. James Reilly was not only a conductor but also one of the pioneers of early jazz development in Canada. In the 1920s, he founded the Elmdale Harmonica Band in Guelph, a groundbreaking move that laid the groundwork for Tommy's harmonica career. After the Great Depression hit in 1929, James Reilly moved to Elmdale Public School as an administrator and in 1930, he created a harmonica band at the school.

The Elgin Regiment Band (photographed in 1930)

During Reilly's childhood, the harmonica was an immensely popular folk instrument in Canada. Whether it was farm workers, railway workers, or street performers, the harmonica was their favorite companion. This small, portable, and inexpensive instrument could produce a rich variety of melodies, making it beloved by people from all walks of life. Young Reilly first encountered the harmonica in the hands of a neighbor's child. The simple folk tune that child played deeply captivated Reilly, and he was immediately enchanted by this magical instrument.

Tommy's musical talent became apparent at a very young age. He not only had a natural affinity for the harmonica but also an astonishing musical memory. After hearing a melody just once, he could roughly imitate it. This talent made his parents realize that their son might have extraordinary potential in music. So, when Tommy was eight years old, his parents decided to have him learn the violin.

1.2 Musical Initiation: Dual Training on Violin and Harmonica

The study of the violin had a profound impact on Reilly. Although the harmonica was his first love, the systematic training on the violin laid a solid foundation for his future musical development. In Canada at that time, the violin was regarded as a "respectable" instrument, and learning it meant receiving formal music education, including sight-singing and ear training, music theory, performance techniques, and more.

Reilly's violin teacher was an immigrant from Europe who brought with him the traditions and rigorous teaching methods of European classical music. Under the guidance of his teacher, Reilly learned the correct bow-holding posture, fingerings, bowing techniques, and how to play beautiful, coherent melodies. Although the training in these fundamentals was tedious, Reilly enjoyed it. He discovered that the techniques of violin playing could be applied to the harmonica, which led to a qualitative leap in his harmonica playing.

While learning the violin, Reilly never gave up his passion for the harmonica. He tried to implement techniques he learned on the violin, such as vibrato, glissando, and legato, on the harmonica. At that time, the harmonica was mainly the ten-hole blues harmonica, with a limited range, but Reilly was already able to play surprisingly complex melodies on this small instrument.

Elmdale Public School's Harmonica Band with Tommy Reilly photo (taken in 1931)

At the age of eleven, he joined the harmonica band founded by his father at Elmdale Public School, becoming the band's featured soloist. This harmonica band was extremely successful at the time, winning numerous awards at the Canadian National Exhibition, and Tommy himself won the solo gold award in both 1932 and 1933. Notably, the chromatic harmonica had just been introduced to the world market by the German company Hohner at that time, representing a completely new type of instrument, and the Reilly family's innovative practices made them important participants in the early history of this instrument.

The trophies and medals won by the Elmdale Harmonica Band

The success of the Elmdale Harmonica Band was not only reflected in its competition results but also in the innovative nature of its educational model. The band organized and systematized harmonica playing, making it an integral part of the school's music education. For the young Tommy, this environment provided valuable ensemble experience and a platform for solo performances. In the band, he played both harmonica and accordion, developing his multi-instrumental skills and adaptability. More importantly, the strict demands of his father as the band conductor instilled in him from a young age the work ethic and technical standards of a professional musician. A key feature of this early training was the emphasis on the "readability" of music – that is, precise performance through sheet music rather than relying solely on aural imitation, which laid the foundation for his future handling of complex classical pieces.

The Evolution of the Harmonica's Role in Classical Music

The history of the harmonica can be traced back to the early 19th century. In 1821, German instrument maker Christian Friedrich Buschmann invented the earliest prototype of the harmonica. Since then, the harmonica underwent numerous improvements and gradually developed into a popular folk instrument. By the late 19th and early 20th centuries, the harmonica was widely popular in the United States and Europe, becoming one of the favorite instruments of the working class and farmers. However, due to its folk instrument origins, the harmonica was long overlooked by the classical music establishment and struggled to gain acceptance in formal settings. In the early 20th century, some musicians began to experiment with introducing the harmonica into the realm of classical music. In the 1920s, American harmonica player John Sebastian Sr. started performing in Europe, playing arrangements of classical music, but he did not attract widespread attention. It was against this backdrop that Reilly's childhood experiences — receiving classical training on the violin while being immersed in the folk traditions of the harmonica — laid the foundation for him to bridge this gap in the future.

1.3 Immigration to the UK and Professional Debut

In 1935, as the Great Depression hit Canada's economy, James Reilly accepted Hohner company's invitation to return to the UK to become the music director of the newly established "Hohner National Song Band League," and the whole family moved to London. This relocation opened up new opportunities for Tommy Reilly's career development.

At just 16 years old, he immediately embarked on a professional performing career, initially as a musician and acrobat in a circus, touring the European continent. This experience, although seemingly far removed from classical music, honed his adaptability to the stage, his ability to improvise, and his professionalism in maintaining performance quality in changing environments. In 1937, he joined a variety show act called "The Four Phillips," performing in variety theatres across Europe, further accumulating stage experience.

Upon arriving in the UK, the Reilly family settled in London. Compared to the tranquility of Canada, the hustle and bustle of London was both exciting and somewhat overwhelming for the young Reilly. However, he quickly adapted to life there and began actively seeking opportunities to develop his music. He continued his violin studies while also not giving up the harmonica.

In 1936, the 17-year-old Reilly made his professional debut in London. It was a performance at a small music hall with a modest audience, but for Reilly, it was a significant starting point in his musical career. He played a series of harmonica solo pieces, including classical music arrangements and folk music. His playing technique and understanding of music left a deep impression on the audience.

The only photo of Tommy Reilly playing the violin

The success of the premiere brought Tommy Reilly more performance opportunities. He began touring variety theaters across Europe as a harmonica soloist. This form of performance was very popular at the time, with audiences eager to see novel acts, and Reilly's harmonica playing perfectly met this demand. He could not only play beautiful melodies but also imitate various sound effects such as bird calls and train whistles, making his performances full of fun and entertainment.

Later, Tommy Reilly toured Europe with "The Four Phillips" - including Germany, Italy, and other places - performing everywhere. He even learned juggling from the troupe members, such as tossing batons, throwing bowls, throwing plates, and even performing while riding on horseback.

In 1939, Reilly made a significant decision: to further his studies in violin at the Leipzig Conservatory in Germany. Leipzig, as the city where Bach had worked and lived for a long time, boasted a profound musical tradition, and its conservatory was one of the oldest and most prestigious music institutions in Europe. This choice indicated that Reilly was still planning to pursue a career as a violinist, with the harmonica being only an auxiliary performance tool. However, the course of history completely altered this plan.

1.4 Father James Reilly: Founder of Harmonica Education in the UK

Portrait of James Reilly (created by Paul Baldwin in February 2024, lecture material from Elgin County Heritage Centre)

If Tommy’s artistic achievements are a big tree, then its roots are deeply embedded in the soil of his father, Captain James Reilly (1886-1955) — his military band conducting background and immigration experience are detailed in section 1.1; this section focuses on his organizational contributions to harmonica education in the UK.

After the Great Depression of 1929, James became the administrator of Elmdale Public School in St. Thomas. In 1930, he founded a harmonica band at the school - it was this band, which won numerous awards at the Canadian National Exhibition, that gave young Tommy his first opportunity to perform as a soloist and established the educational foundation of "precise playing from sheet music" for both father and son. In 1935, James accepted an invitation from the Hohner company to return to the UK, where he took up the position of Music Director for the newly formed "Hohner Song Band League" (HSBL), an appointment that also brought the 16-year-old Tommy back to Britain.

Over the next 20 years, James dedicated himself entirely to harmonica education in the UK: writing systematic teaching materials for the association, composing band classics like "Windsor March," serving as a youth trainer and examiner for the British Accordion Academy; after WWII, he became the sales manager of Hohner's music division, inviting numerous composers to create new works for harmonica and accordion, establishing the largest harmonica music catalog in the UK; in the early 1950s, he formed the Hohner employee band. He served as vice-chairman of the association and later the National Harmonica League (NHL) for many years. He retired in 1954 and passed away the following year at the age of 69. Dr. Meyer, the general manager of Hohner UK, said in his eulogy: During his 20 years as music director, most harmonica players in the UK received their education through the teaching materials he wrote, the guidance he provided, and the music he published.

The relay between two generations formed the complete genealogy of harmonica education in the UK: the father spent twenty years establishing the organizational and textbook framework for harmonica in Britain (HSBL founded in 1935, reorganized as NHL in 1951, and after independence in 1981, founding the magazine Harmonica World); the son devoted his life to constructing a technical system, classical repertoire, and international teaching network for the harmonica. By the time his father passed away in 1955, Tommy was already a household name as a radio star in the UK; twelve years later, within the Hohner system where his father once served, Tommy created the world's first concert harmonica - the harmonica revolution of two generations was completed at this moment.

Chapter 2: Perseverance Amidst the Flames of War: The POW Camp Years (1939-1945)

2.1 Outbreak of War and Arrest

On September 1, 1939, Nazi Germany invaded Poland, officially marking the start of World War II; in the same month, Britain and Canada declared war on Germany in succession. As a British citizen, Reilly's situation in Germany immediately became perilous. In August 1939, on the eve of the war, the 20-year-old Tommy Reilly, still harboring youthful dreams and studying at the Leipzig Conservatory in Germany, was arrested as a hostage by the Gestapo at his hotel under the pretext of being an enemy alien (some say on September 3).

"Music is the language of the soul, and the harmonica is my medium of expression." — Tommy Reilly

Speaking of this arrest, there is an interesting or rather tragic story. One night, Tommy Reilly was about to go on a blind date and was preparing to meet a girl from the German National Ballet. Just as he was leaving his hotel after checking out, the Gestapo arrived and he was arrested. Interestingly, 15 years later, during a performance, a lady came backstage and said they had never met before, and she was the ballet dancer from that time. Later, when talking about this incident, Tommy Reilly couldn't help but lament that what should have been a happy memory turned into an unforgettable arrest experience.

This was not a simple detention, but a harrowing ordeal of being held hostage and moved from place to place. Reilly was imprisoned in POW camps in Germany, Poland, France, and other locations. The conditions in these camps were dire, life was tough, and more importantly, as a hostage, he faced extreme uncertainty about the future — not knowing what would happen the next day, when the war would end, or even if he would survive.

For the young Reilly, this was a devastating blow. His burgeoning music career was abruptly halted, his freedom taken away, and his future became bleak. However, it was in these extremely harsh conditions that Reilly demonstrated an astonishing willpower and dedication to music. He did not let the adversity defeat him; instead, he turned the prisoner-of-war camp into his own "music academy."

2.2 Moving Between Prisoner-of-War Camps and Music as a Spiritual Pillar

After being arrested by the Gestapo, he was first sent to a thousand-year-old castle in Weissenburg, Bavaria. About two years later, he received his luggage, which also contained several harmonicas.

Reilly's experience in prisoner-of-war camps lasted for five years and eight months, a duration far beyond what most people could imagine. He was imprisoned in various POW camps in Germany, Poland, and France, facing extremely harsh material conditions and continuous mental oppression. Life in the camps was incredibly tough: food was scarce, living quarters were overcrowded, sanitary conditions were appalling, and prisoners had to endure heavy physical labor. Many prisoners were exhausted physically and mentally in this environment, and some even lost the will to live.

The specific details of this period were rarely elaborated on by Reilly to outsiders, but one can imagine that for a 20-year-old young man at a critical stage of music learning, being deprived of the ability to play the violin was a tremendous mental torment.

However, Reilly found his way to survive in such an environment - through music. More importantly, he managed to exchange coffee from Canadian Red Cross food parcels with a Canadian-born German supply officer, eventually obtaining two dozen (24) new Hohner harmonicas, including the Hohner 270 Chromatica chromatic harmonica. At that time, Hohner harmonicas had wooden combs, and the old ones he had were mostly swollen and cracked, making them unplayable. The German Hohner company was also the only manufacturer of harmonicas at the time. Many people do not know that the postwar German Hohner company was on the verge of bankruptcy due to financing issues. This supply officer, who loved classical music, not only provided him with harmonicas but also supplied broadcast equipment and music manuscript paper.

Reilly's choice to trade coffee—a luxury extremely scarce during wartime—for a harmonica indicated that he had already prioritized music practice over immediate physical needs. This choice foreshadowed a core characteristic of his later artistic career: an absolute pursuit of musical perfectionism and a firm determination to elevate harmonica playing to the highest artistic level. According to records, he practiced for several hours every day, "honing his skills as if playing a violin," treating the harmonica with the same seriousness as the violin. This attitude was extremely rare in the harmonica-playing community at the time, as most players regarded the harmonica as an entertaining, auxiliary instrument rather than a professional tool requiring systematic technical training.

As is well known in the music world, it was during these years in the prisoner-of-war camp that Tommy Reilly honed his harmonica skills to perfection. Driven by his ambition to become a violinist, Reilly approached the harmonica with the mindset of a violinist—a way of thinking across instruments that is unique in the history of the harmonica.

Reilly secretly listened to broadcasts in the prisoner-of-war camp, including performances by the violin virtuoso Jascha Heifetz. Heifetz (1901-1987) is recognized as one of the greatest violinists of the 20th century, known for his flawless technique, precise intonation, distinctive tone, and strong musical personality. He meticulously studied the playing of his favorite violinist, Jascha Heifetz, particularly Heifetz's signature continuous, rich vibrato, perfect intonation control, and singing tone. Reilly attempted to recreate the tonal beauty of the violin on the harmonica, an instrument with metal reeds.

For Reilly, Heifetz's playing was not only an aesthetic enjoyment but also a model for technical study. He systematically analyzed Heifetz's vibrato technique, fingerings, phrasing, and tone control, and pondered how to transfer these techniques to the harmonica.

This pursuit shaped several core characteristics of Reilly's future playing style:

Singing Tone: Pursuing a warm tone similar to the human voice, rather than the common metallic timbre of the harmonica

Continuous vibrato: The almost uninterrupted use of vibrato, imitating the string expression of a violin

Perfect Pitch Control: An almost fanatical demand for the accuracy of each note

Prioritizing Musicality: Technique Always Serves Musical Expression, Not Showing Off

This experience not only shaped his technique but also his musical philosophy: in extreme environments, music is the meaning of survival; in a free world, music should be an honest expression of emotion.

2.3 Advancements in Harmonica Technique: Establishment of a Technical System

Reilly met a pianist named Frank Still in the prisoner-of-war camp. Still later became Reilly's regular accompanist, and the two collaborated for many years. This friendship forged in the prisoner-of-war camp had a significant impact on Reilly's post-war musical development.

In the prisoner-of-war camp, Reilly had ample time to focus on harmonica practice. He systematically applied the knowledge and techniques acquired from his violin studies to the harmonica, conducting groundbreaking research. He analyzed the principles of harmonica sound production, breath control, fingering techniques, and gradually established a complete set of classical harmonica performance techniques.

The Core Techniques Developed by Reilly in the Prisoner-of-War Camp

Breath Control: In violin playing, the pressure and speed of the bow directly affect the tone and volume. Reilly applied this concept to the harmonica, achieving subtle variations in tone and precise control of volume by regulating the intensity, speed, and stability of his exhalation and inhalation. From a physics perspective, the vibration frequency of the harmonica's reeds is proportional to the speed of the airflow, while the volume is related to the flow rate of the air.

Vibrato Technique: Vibrato is an important means of expressing emotion in violin playing. Reilly discovered that by controlling the regular fluctuation of breath—usually within a minor second, with a frequency of about 5-7 times per second—a similar vibrato effect to that of a violin can be achieved on the harmonica. This fluctuation should come from the control of the diaphragm, not from the movements of the throat or tongue.

Legato and Staccato: The violin has a rich variety of bowing techniques, such as legato, détaché, and spiccato. Reilly studied how to achieve similar articulation on the harmonica by controlling the interruption and connection of airflow, as well as coordinating the tongue and throat, to produce various playing effects.

Bending: Although the harmonica is a "fixed pitch" instrument, Reilly discovered that by controlling the angle and force of breath, the pitch of the harmonica can be finely adjusted. This technique is particularly important when playing melodic pieces, allowing the music to be more expressive.

Reilly's efforts to transfer violin techniques to the harmonica were systematic and creative. In terms of vibrato techniques, he developed various harmonica-specific vibrato types, including jaw vibrato, throat vibrato, and abdominal vibrato, to simulate the rich variations of violin vibrato. In tone control, he studied the relationship between breath pressure, oral cavity shape, and reed response, achieving a spectrum of tones from clarinet-like dark to flute-like bright. In phrasing, he translated the concept of violin bowing into harmonica breath control, creating harmonica equivalents of "legato" and "staccato."

Reilly's vibrato technique was unparalleled in its refinement during his time. Singaporean harmonica player Cheng Jang Ming, in analyzing his recordings, specifically noted: "I actually prefer his vibrato from his early years, more intense, more violin-like", indicating that his vibrato had a strong "violin-like" quality - that is, a highly controlled, expressive vibrato effect. Even more astonishing was his throat staccato technique, which allowed him to achieve "so incredibly fast and yet distinctly clean" results when playing rapid passages. In pieces like Red Flame and Hora Staccato, he was even able to perform octave double stops with throat staccato at high speeds, a technical feat considered unimaginable at the time.

2.4 Experiences in the Prisoner of War Camp: A Part of Life

Tommy Reilly's musical practice in the prisoner-of-war camp was not conducted in isolation; he actively participated in the camp's musical life. He played for his fellow prisoners, organized musical activities, and collaborated with other musicians. These practices not only maintained his playing condition but also developed his stage presence and audience communication skills. In the extremely oppressive environment, music became an important avenue for spiritual liberation, and Tommy Reilly's performances undoubtedly provided much-needed psychological solace to many prisoners.

It was not until the spring of 1945, just before the end of World War II, that Reilly was rescued by British forces in Lüneburg, northern Germany, according to Tommy Reilly's son David Reilly, it was likely the Scottish army. In total, Reilly spent 5 years and 8 months in the prisoner-of-war camp, making him one of the longest-held prisoners of war during World War II. Of course, Tommy Reilly did not give up on escaping; he planned to escape three times, but each time he was caught, and once he was nearly shot dead by a German officer.

When he walked out of the prison camp gate, he tightly clutched the harmonica that had accompanied him through six difficult years. This harmonica witnessed the growth of a musician and a journey of indomitable spirit. After the war, Reilly recalled this period: "In those dark days, the harmonica was my only friend. It kept me sane and made me believe that one day I would regain my freedom and continue my musical dreams."

Ironically, according to Tommy Reilly's son, throughout his entire time as a POW during World War II, Tommy Reilly's violin was never confiscated by the German military. I personally speculate that it was due to his musical talent that he received preferential treatment from German officers. After World War II ended, Tommy Reilly was repatriated to the UK. However, he never got his violin back; it was presumably stolen by British soldiers at the time. As a result, Tommy Reilly later said, "I will never play the violin again."

After the war, he received £1 as war reparations — a sum that seems trivial today, but which symbolized the end of an era back then. A journalist once asked him after the war if he felt that he had wasted six years of his life in the prisoner-of-war camp.

Reilly's response showed his extraordinary attitude towards life:

"These years in the prison camp were part of my life, not taken out of my life."

Despite this unpleasant experience, Reilly did not hold a grudge against Germany. On the contrary, he was passionate about reading World War II history, and he didn't miss any books on the subject available on the market. Perhaps, he wanted to understand through learning history why he had encountered such a fate.

Radio in Britain: The Broadcasting Landscape of 1945-1960

To understand Reilly's rapid rise to fame after the war, one must understand the media environment he entered. In 1945 Britain, television was not yet widespread (it was the 1953 coronation of Elizabeth II that truly brought TV into people's homes), radio was the absolute mass medium. The BBC operated as a public monopoly, with its "Home Service" and "Light Programme" covering everything from news and drama to light music; evening prime-time variety shows had millions of listeners.

Reilly was right in the middle of this light music-variety show assembly line: Variety Bandbox (variety show) and Workers Playtime (a program often broadcast live in factory canteens) were the most popular formats of the time - a mix of light music, classical pieces, comedy, and vocal performances. These shows demanded a lot from the performers: live broadcasts, one-take recordings, a vast repertoire, and a wide stylistic range (classical arrangements juxtaposed with popular tunes). Reilly's "crossover ability" thrived in this environment: he could play Bach's Badinerie in one segment and immediately follow it with El Cumbanchero.

The broadcasting ecosystem also shaped his recording patterns: during the 1950s-1960s, he made numerous broadcast recordings (most unreleased commercially), with over a dozen BBC collaborating orchestras documented in the Groven catalog alone; the success of the 1953 Stuttgart "Light Music Week" immediately translated into invitations from continental European radio and television – the post-war European broadcasting symphony orchestras (nine in Germany, five in the Nordic countries, and others in the Netherlands, Belgium, Switzerland, and France) represented a larger performance market than commercial records, and Reilly's records of collaborations with dozens of these orchestras (see Appendix F) are a product of this ecosystem.

Worth noting historically: after his first BBC radio performance around 1947, he received a flood of listener mail; and his radio career spanned the entire media evolution from the 78 rpm era to stereo LPs and then to TV variety shows - in the 1960s he even turned the theme song of The Navy Lark into a radio memory familiar to all of Britain (the show aired from 1959-1977, with the harmonica theme song accompanying an entire generation of Britons).

In 1945, the Second World War ended, and Tommy Reilly returned to Britain. Six years in a prisoner-of-war camp had transformed him from a young music student into a mature musician. Although his body was weakened by long-term malnutrition, his musical skills had reached a new height.

At the age of 25, Tommy Reilly returned to London and began his career as a professional harmonica player. However, the initial path was not smooth. At that time, the harmonica music scene already had two giants: Larry Adler and John Sebastian, whose careers were both at their zenith.

Larry Adler was known for his astonishing technique and stage charisma, bringing the harmonica into classical concert halls; John Sebastian was respected for his profound musicality and identity as a composer. Reilly, as a newcomer, had to find his own unique position.

Reilly first began performing in clubs, pubs, and small music halls across the UK. His playing style was unique, combining the rigor and depth of classical music with the warmth and vitality of folk music. In the late 1940s, Reilly's career reached a significant turning point - he started performing on BBC programs.

He participated in popular programs such as "Variety Bandbox" and "Workers Playtime," which featured a mix of light music, classical excerpts, and entertaining content, perfectly aligning with Reilly's ability to cross musical genres. These radio performances not only boosted his public profile but also honed his skills in adapting to different musical styles and performance environments.

Around 1947, Reilly's first BBC radio performance received an enthusiastic response from listeners, with the BBC receiving numerous letters asking who this "magical harmonica player" was. Subsequently, Reilly became a regular on the BBC, frequently appearing on various music programs, and became a household name.

In the early post-war period, Reilly joined the Clarkson Rose musical theater troupe's tour, an experience that continued his work mode in variety theater but provided a larger performance platform and more professional production standards. The musical theater troupe's performances required musicians to have a wide repertoire, the ability to switch pieces quickly, and the coordination with other performers such as singers and dancers. 3.2 The Scientific Basis of His Playing Style

Reilly's playing style, the most notable characteristic of which was applying violin techniques and concepts to the harmonica, originated from his early experience learning the violin and his in-depth research while in a prisoner-of-war camp. He particularly admired the violin master Jascha Heifetz, and had attempted to learn Heifetz's techniques such as vibrato and tremolo.

The Application of Breath Dynamics

Reilly's breath control technique can be analyzed from a fluid dynamics perspective. The vibration of the harmonica's reeds requires a certain amount of pressure and airflow: pressure determines the amplitude of the reed's vibration (volume), while airflow determines the duration of the vibration (note length). Reilly achieved precise control over volume and note length by precisely controlling the speed and flow rate of his exhalation and inhalation.

When playing long notes, Reilly was able to maintain absolute stability in his airflow, which required exceptional control of his respiratory muscles. His diaphragm, intercostal muscles, and abdominal muscles worked in harmony to maintain constant air pressure. This control was akin to the bow control he developed while learning the violin in his early years.

Adjustment of the Oral Resonance Cavity

Reilly paid great attention to the regulation of the oral cavity as a resonator. The tone of the harmonica is not only determined by the vibration of the reeds but is also influenced by oral resonance. By changing the shape and size of the oral cavity, Reilly was able to adjust the resonance frequency, thereby altering the tone:

Large oral cavity: Produces a warmer, rounder tone with richer low-frequency overtones, suitable for playing soft, lyrical passages.

Small oral cavity space: Produces a brighter, sharper tone with more prominent high-frequency overtones, suitable for playing passionate and bright passages.

The Articulation Technique of the Tongue

In Reilly's articulation technique, the tongue plays a crucial role. Through rapid movements of the tongue, he was able to achieve various effects:

Legato: Maintain continuous airflow, keep the tongue relaxed, and let the sound flow smoothly.

Hard Staccato: The tongue quickly cuts off the airflow to produce short and powerful notes.

Soft Staccato: The tongue movement is gentle, with notes that are short but not overly sharp.

Portato: Between legato and staccato, with slight separation between notes but maintaining duration.

3.3 Establishing a Unique Playing Style

By the early 1950s, Reilly had established his own unique playing style. His performances combined the rigor and depth of classical music with the warmth and vitality of folk music; showcasing both exquisite technical skills and profound emotional expression.

His style can be summarized with the following keywords: lyrical - the melodic lines are clear and flowing, as if someone is singing; precision - every note's duration, dynamics, and timbre are meticulously controlled; expressiveness - adept at conveying a wide range of emotions through music; diversity - his repertoire spans from Baroque to modern music.

Reilly's way of holding the harmonica reflects the influence of violin playing posture. He holds the chromatic harmonica at a near-horizontal angle in front of him, with his left hand stabilizing the instrument and his right hand mimicking the motion of a violin bow, moving near the body of the harmonica to assist with breath control. This posture not only has a professional visual appeal—distinguishing it from the casual holding of folk harmonica playing—but more importantly, it achieves precise technical control. The stability of the left hand ensures a reliable contact between the mouthpiece and the lips, reducing the risk of air leakage and unstable pitch; the assisting motion of the right hand helps regulate breath pressure and direction, achieving tone control similar to bow variations.

The implementation of glissando and legato techniques is a key component of this technical system. Reilly developed various techniques to achieve violin-like glissando (portamento) and legato effects. The glissando technique achieves a smooth transition between pitches by changing the shape of the mouth and the angle of airflow, without using the keys; the legato technique, through precise breath control and key operation, eliminates the break between notes, creating a flowing melodic line.

4.3 Concerto In-Depth Music Analysis

Spivakovsky's Harmonica Concerto has three movements, each with its own unique charm and technical challenges:

First Movement: Fuocoso - Danse Drôle (Passionate - Comical Dance)

The structure of this movement can be subdivided into the following sections:

Orchestral Introduction (bars 1-10): Begins with a broad orchestral passage, primarily using strings and woodwinds. The music is in A minor, with a steady rhythm and a solemn atmosphere, reminiscent of the style of Sibelius.

Harmonica Cadenza (bars 11-38): This is an innovative move—placing an unaccompanied solo cadenza so early in the piece. The harmonica enters with an unaccompanied cadenza, featuring rapid scale runs, wide interval leaps, grace notes, and more, allowing the soloist to introduce his instrument to the concert audience in an expressive and technically impressive manner.

First Theme and Second Theme (Bars 38-96):

The band re-enters, presenting the lively and powerful first theme. The music then transitions to C major, introducing a more lyrical and beautiful second theme, where the harmonica showcases its singing quality.

Development and Recapitulation (bars 97-192): The earlier material is developed and varied, showcasing demanding techniques such as rapid scales, arpeggios, and double stops. Finally, the first and second themes reappear, concluding with an energetic coda.

Second Movement: Romance, Andante dolce (Romance, Sweet Andante)

This slow movement is filled with lyrical and romantic atmosphere:

Theme Presentation (Bars 1-24): The harmonica solo presents a beautiful and flowing theme, with the piano providing simple harmonic accompaniment.

Middle Duet (bars 25-48): This is the highlight of the movement. A solo violin in a high register joins the harmonica, and the two instruments form a beautiful duet, creating a unique sound effect.

Theme Recapitulation (Bars 49-72):

The theme reappears but with some embellishments and variations, ultimately concluding with a gentle coda.

Third Movement: Scherzo, Allegro ma non troppo (Scherzo, Not Too Fast)

This is a fast, lively movement, full of technique and vitality:

The harmonica presents a lively and skipping theme, followed by a technical display section, including rapid scale runs, wide interval leaps, varied articulation, and more. The middle section of the music shifts to a slower tempo, creating a more lyrical atmosphere, providing a contrast to the preceding technical display. Finally, the theme reappears, concluding the piece with an enthusiastic coda.

4.4 The Influence of the Harmonica Concerto on Reilly's Playing Art

The world premiere of this concerto was accompanied by the London Concert Orchestra with Mark Lubbock conducting, a collaboration that was highly symbolic in itself — the Concert Orchestra, as a professional ensemble under the BBC, broke the instrumental prejudice of orchestras by partnering with a harmonica soloist, proving that the harmonica could effectively interact with a symphony orchestra in terms of volume, timbre, and technical expression.

Michael Spivakovsky, an important violinist and composer, chose to compose a harmonica concerto for Reilly, which in itself indicated that Reilly had already established a certain reputation in classical music circles. This 1951 concerto was not only technically demanding, but more importantly, it required the harmonica to engage in an equal dialogue with the symphony orchestra as a solo instrument, which was very avant-garde at the time. The successful premiere of this work established Reilly's status as a classical harmonica player, rather than just a pop harmonica player. From then on, his career truly began to change. More and more composers were willing to write pieces for him, and formal performance invitations—including collaborations with symphony orchestras—kept coming in.

Reilly later recalled, "My playing underwent a huge transformation starting in 1951 when Michael Spivakovsky composed a concerto for me. The months I spent with him taught me a great deal. It was not until then that I realized how much potential the harmonica had. Although Michael Spivakovsky couldn't play the harmonica, he instinctively knew what could be done on the instrument, and he made me do it. He was a tough taskmaster, but I will always be grateful to him for 'bullying' me."

This experience also shaped Reilly's future teaching methods. Like Spivakovsky, he had a clear vision for the interpretation of musical pieces, and he would not be satisfied until the student approached his idea in every detail.

1951 was also a pivotal year in Reilly's recording career. He signed with the Parlophone label, with recordings produced by the young George Martin. However, a careful review of Reilly's discography from 1951 to 1971 reveals an interesting phenomenon: until 1971, with the exception of a classical album he recorded for Hohner, all of Reilly's records were primarily focused on contemporary pop songs and Latin music.

This twenty-year career in commercial recordings shows that although Reilly performed concertos in classical concerts, in the recording studio, he mainly recorded light music, pop song arrangements, and Latin dance music. 5.1 Gordon Jacob and the Work Five Pieces

After the success of the Spivakovsky Concerto, Reilly began actively seeking collaborations with other composers. One of the most significant of these was with British composers.

Gordon Jacob was one of the most important British composers, orchestration experts, and music educators of the 20th century. Born on July 5, 1895, in Upper Norwood, London, Jacob served in the infantry during World War I and began teaching himself composition by reading harmony textbooks in the prison camp library after being captured in 1917, organizing a prison camp "orchestra" to perform for his fellow inmates. After the war, he enrolled at the Royal College of Music (RCM) in 1920, studying under Charles Villiers Stanford, Ralph Vaughan Williams (composition), Herbert Howells (theory), and Adrian Boult (conducting).

From 1924 to his retirement in 1966, Jacob taught at the RCM, instructing in composition, orchestration, and music theory, and nurturing renowned musicians such as Malcolm Arnold, Imogen Holst, and Cyril Smith. He was an authority in orchestration, authoring classic textbooks like Orchestral Technique: A Manual for Students (1931) and The Elements of Orchestration (1962), which are still widely used today. As a composer, he created over 700 works throughout his life, spanning symphonies, concertos, ballets, film scores, and a vast array of wind ensemble pieces.

The Collaboration Between Gordon Jacob and Tommy Reilly

Jacob's collaboration with Tommy Reilly began in the mid-1950s. As an expert in orchestration, Jacob had a profound understanding of the capabilities of wind instruments, which provided a technical foundation for composing for the harmonica as a unique instrument. Jacob highly praised Reilly's artistic achievements: "He made the harmonica into a solo instrument of high artistic worth."

In addition to Five Pieces, Jacob's compositions for harmonica also include Divertimento for Harmonica and String Quartet (1955, dedicated to Larry Adler, premiered by Adler in November 1956; Reilly recorded it with the Hindar Quartet in 1975) and Introduction and Galop (featured on the 1976 album Music for Two Harmonicas for two harmonicas). These works formed the core of Reilly's early repertoire and laid the foundation for the harmonica's position in the classical music world.

The Musical Language and Style Characteristics of Five Pieces

Jacob's musical style is described as "firmly based upon the traditions," and he believes in the importance of melody, stating that "the day that melody is discarded altogether, you may as well pack up music." In the Five Pieces, this aesthetic is manifested as:

(1) Melody First Principle: Each movement is centered around a clearly identifiable thematic melody, with the lyrical lines of the second movement Cradle Song particularly showcasing the harmonica's ability to mimic the human voice.

(2) Folk music elements: The third movement, Country Dance, incorporates rhythmic characteristics of English folk dance (such as 6/8 time or syncopation), while the fifth movement, Russian Dance, employs strong dotted rhythms and Phrygian mode elements, imitating the Russian style of composers like Balakirev or Tchaikovsky.

Orchestral Color: As a master of orchestration, Jacob demonstrated superb skill in writing for string accompaniments. Critics pointed out: "Jacob scores wonderfully well for wind instruments; he produces such variety in tone color that the sound never cloys." In the Five Pieces, the string section's techniques of pizzicato, con sordino, and divisi create a delicate dialogue with the harmonica's breathiness.

Toledo - James Moody's Masterpiece

The full name of the piece Toledo is Toledo, Spanish Fantasy for Harmonica and Orchestra, an important work composed by British composer James Moody in 1960 for the renowned chromatic harmonica player Tommy Reilly. The piece blends Spanish folk music elements with modern playing techniques, fully showcasing the expressive capabilities of the chromatic harmonica. It is a representative work in the harmonica solo repertoire of the mid-20th century and remains a standard piece in international harmonica competitions and concerts.

"Toledo," composed in 1960, belongs to the "Mini Piano Concerto/Tabloid Concerto" genre, with a duration of about 6 minutes and 43 seconds. The piece is dedicated to Tommy Reilly and quickly became a repertoire staple for him. As a piece specifically tailored for the chromatic harmonica, Toledo has the following technical characteristics:

Robert Farnon (1917-2005) was born in Canada and settled in the UK after World War II, becoming a renowned arranger and composer. Robert Joseph Farnon was recognized as the "greatest composer of light orchestral music in the world" in the second half of the 20th century. Farnon discovered the unique musical genre of "Light Music" in the UK and combined it with the fresh vitality of North America, breathing new life into this musical form that might have faded in the 1950s. He not only composed a vast amount of radio music for the BBC but also scored films (such as Colditz, Captain Horatio Hornblower R.N., etc.) and collaborated with music legends like Frank Sinatra and Oscar Peterson. "Prelude and Dance" was composed in 1966 (though some sources cite the mid-1960s) specifically by Farnon to showcase Reilly's extraordinary talent. According to Tommy Reilly's interview in 1973, the creation of this piece became a pivotal turning point in the history of harmonica development:

Analysis of the Musical Style of Prelude and Dance

Based on the analysis of existing recordings and literature, Prelude and Dance adopts a typical two-movement contrasting structure, consistent with the title indications of "Prelude" and "Dance":

| Section | Characteristics | Musical Personality | Technical Points | | Prelude | Slower introductory section, lyrical | Melodic, showcasing the harmonica's "vocal" quality | Long note control, breath continuity, tone purity | | Dance | Fast main section, rhythmic | Lively dance rhythm, possibly incorporating jazz elements | Rapid tonguing, octave jumps, complex rhythmic patterns |

This structure is reminiscent of the Baroque period's "Prelude and Fugue" or "Prelude and Dance" suite forms, but Farnon imbues it with the typical mid-20th century light orchestral music color. The entire piece lasts about 10 minutes and 32 seconds, belonging to the medium concerto genre, and is more expansive than James Moody's Toledo (approximately 6 minutes and 43 seconds).

As one of Farnon's masterpieces, this piece embodies his signature string writing style. Farnon's orchestration is known for its "luscious musical bed," and he excels at creating a rich yet unobtrusive orchestral backdrop for soloists. According to the 1971 recording information, the piece was accompanied by the "Robert Farnon Orchestra" (Farnon's own orchestra), ensuring a unified stylistic understanding of the work.

The orchestration might include: a full string section, woodwinds (flute, oboe, clarinet, bassoon), brass (horns, trumpet, trombone), and percussion. This setup is closer to a chamber orchestra than a simple "light music" ensemble, reflecting Farnon's respect for Reilly's artistic stature.

Farnon's compositions blend a variety of musical elements:

Classical Tradition: The works reflect the harmonic influences of Tchaikovsky, Ravel, Debussy, and even Sibelius;

Jazz Influence: Farnon's friendships with jazz greats like Dizzy Gillespie, Oscar Peterson, and others imbued his work with swing rhythms and a sense of improvisation;

Film Music Techniques: Farnon's experience scoring over 40 films gives his work a strong sense of visual imagery and dramatic tension;

Canadian National Elements: As a Canadian, Farnon often infused his works with a sense of longing for his homeland, although Prelude and Dance primarily showcases an international light music vocabulary.

Analysis of Technical Difficulties in Prelude and Dance

According to Reilly's own assessment, this piece has extremely high technical and musical demands. Combining Farnon's compositional style with Reilly's playing technique, the following technical challenges can be inferred:

Breath Control and Sustaining Long Notes

The prelude section may contain long, flowing melodies, requiring the performer to have a bow-like legato feel reminiscent of Jascha Heifetz's violin playing. Reilly developed his harmonica technique during his time as a prisoner of war, using Heifetz's records as a model, with a particular focus on smooth, connected (legato) transitions between notes.

(2) Fast Technique and Rhythmic Precision: The dance sections may include fast-paced patterns similar to "Jumping Bean," demanding precise tonguing and slide techniques. Farnon, having been a trumpeter, had very high standards for rhythmic precision.

Timbre Variation and Dynamic Contrast:

Farnon's compositions typically demand a rich dynamic range (from pp to ff), and the standard harmonica has limitations in this regard. This is one of the reasons Reilly sought silver harmonicas – the silver material produces a brighter, more penetrating tone with a wider dynamic range.

Speaking of the prelude, the opening two notes—D and A, from hole 5 inhaling to hole 7 inhaling—require a very smooth transition to achieve a legato effect. Reilly once said, "Of all the music I've played, I have to say that the opening two notes of Farnon's piece give me more satisfaction than almost any other notes. When there is a hole between each note, playing two notes legato is not easy. Achieving this and making it sound as if there is no gap between the two notes is a real accomplishment."

The dance section employs a 6/8 time signature, fast and forceful, utilizing a multitude of eighth and sixteenth notes. This is one of the most technically challenging parts in harmonica literature, including rapid scale runs, wide interval leaps, and intricate rhythmic patterns.

Reilly recounted an amusing anecdote about Farnon on a BBC program: "Bob Farnon wrote a serious piece for me. It was so difficult to play that I felt he wrote it hoping I wouldn't be able to perform it. Finally, I told Bob Farnon over the phone, 'Bob, I've got your piece down now.' Bob Farnon replied, 'Play me the last few bars.' After the performance, Bob Farnon said, 'Great, Tommy, but double the speed.'"

In 1971, Richard Hayman, conductor of the Boston Pops Orchestra, wrote after receiving the LP featuring Prelude and Dance: "Tommy's interpretation of Farnon's work is absolutely breathtaking; played so perfectly and masterfully that I can scarcely believe my ears. I am truly thrilled by this magnificent display of the harmonica's incredible capabilities in the hands of this great performer."

Analysis of the Influence of Prelude and Dance

Prelude and Dance and James Moody's Toledo are regarded as the two most representative "non-full concerto" works (i.e., single-movement or two-movement concerto-style pieces) in Tommy Reilly's repertoire. If Toledo represents a virtuosic piece with Spanish flair, Prelude and Dance embodies a more symphonic, mature, and refined light music concerto style.

The piece was first recorded in 1971 on the Polydor album "The Music of Robert Farnon" (catalog number 2382 008), and was re-recorded in 1993 by Chandos (CHAN 9248), included in the album "Tommy Reilly Plays Harmonica Concertos", alongside complete concertos by Spivakovsky, Arnold, and Villa-Lobos, highlighting its importance in Reilly's artistic career.

The Prelude and Dance can also be said to have indirectly promoted the specialization of the harmonica manufacturing industry. The Hohner company put the Silver Concerto into mass production in 1968-1969, and although it was expensive and made-to-order, it established the concept of a "professional concert harmonica." Subsequently, companies like Yamaha also began to focus on the high-end harmonica market, and Reilly's student Sigmund Groven later collaborated with Norwegian engineer Georg Pollestad to develop the "Polle Concert Harmonica" (launched in 1981), which can also be regarded as a continuation of this tradition.

Farnon demonstrated through Prelude and Dance that light music is not "frivolous music," but rather a genre with a high degree of artistry and technical skill. As critics have noted, Farnon's work is "handcrafted and melodious," and like classical masters such as Mozart and Beethoven, should not be forgotten due to changing fashions. This piece earned respect for the harmonica in the classical music world, proving that this "folk instrument" is equally capable of serious musical expression.

Robert Farnon's Prelude and Dance for Harmonica and Orchestra is a dual milestone in the history of the harmonica in the 20th century: it is both a work of high artistic value and a catalyst for innovations in instrument manufacturing technology. Through this piece, Farnon not only demonstrated his prowess in orchestral writing as the "master of light music," but also propelled the harmonica's transformation from a "folk toy" to a "serious solo instrument."

The Silver Concerto harmonica, inspired by his work, remains Hohner's flagship product, used by classical harmonica players worldwide. Tommy Reilly, through this piece and the instrumental innovation it sparked, further solidified his place in harmonica history, paving the way for receiving the Member of the Order of the British Empire (MBE) in 1992—becoming the first harmonica player to be honored with this award.

For today's harmonica players and researchers, Prelude and Dance is not only a technical challenge to overcome but also an important case study for understanding the mid-20th-century "light music" aesthetic, the professionalization of the harmonica, and transatlantic musical cultural exchange. It reminds us that the development of instruments is often closely linked to the demands of specific pieces, and that great artists (like Farnon and Reilly) always manage to carve out new possibilities at the intersection of technique and art.

5.4 Important Works by Other British Composers

One of Tommy Reilly's most significant institutional contributions was the establishment of the composer commissioning model. Starting from the Spivakovsky Concerto in 1951, he created a complete chain where performers actively commission, composers specifically create, and works are dedicated to the performers. This model has been widely adopted by later generations. Over 30 concerto-like works were composed for him, constituting the core of harmonica concertos.

Of course, there are also harmonica concertos that were not specifically composed for Tommy Reilly but were recorded or performed by him, such as concertos written for Larry Adler. However, Tommy Reilly still left behind classic recordings or live performances.

Concerto for Harmonica and Orchestra, Op. 46 (1954)

Malcolm Arnold (1921-2006) was one of the most important British composers of the 20th century, known for his nine symphonies and numerous concertos. His Harmonica Concerto (Op. 46), composed in 1954, was commissioned by the BBC for that year's Proms and dedicated to Larry Adler; Reilly included it in his repertoire and left a classic recording. This work showcases Arnold's "poetic side," and unlike his grand orchestral pieces, this concerto, written for a small chamber orchestra, fully demonstrates the "musical qualities" of the harmonica.

The three movements of the piece are: I. Grazioso (gracefully), II. Mesto (sadly), III. Con Brio (with vigor). The second movement "Mesto" (sad) reflects Arnold's profound introspective nature, while the final movement returns to his signature rhythmic vitality. The work was recorded by Reilly with the London Sinfonietta, conducted by David Atherton.

Romance for Harmonica, Strings and Piano (1951)

Ralph Vaughan Williams (1872-1958), a prominent figure of the British folk music school, composed the Romance in D-flat for harmonica, strings, and piano in 1951. This piece was originally written for Larry Adler (premiered in New York on May 3, 1952), but Reilly also included it in his repertoire and recorded it.

The piece adopts a single-movement form, with a gentle and nostalgic style, "like a memory of simpler times, perhaps a quiet evening by the fireside." Vaughan Williams employs his signature English pastoral style, with rich string textures and the harmonica playing a lyrical melody. Although this work was not premiered by Reilly, his rendition (with the Academy of St Martin in the Fields and Neville Marriner) has become a classic recording.

Other Harmonica Concerto Works

Vilem Tausky (1910-2004): Czech-British conductor and composer, who wrote the Concertino for Harmonica and Orchestra (three movements: Allegro Moderato, Nocturne, Scherzo), featured on Reilly's album with the Academy of St Martin in the Fields.

Graham Whettam (1927-2007): Composed the Fantasy for Harmonica and Orchestra.

Matyas Seiber (1905-1960): A Hungarian-born British composer who wrote Old Scottish Air for Harmonica, Strings and Harp.

George Martin (1926-2016): The famous Beatles producer and Reilly's producer at Parlophone Records, who composed Three American Sketches for Harmonica and Strings and Adagietto for Harmonica and Strings.

Paul Patterson (1947-): British modernist composer who wrote Propositions for Harmonica and Strings for Reilly in 1987. The Times review stated that this work proved Reilly's "determination to establish the 'highbrow' credentials of his silver instrument, matching his skill in coaxing lyrical, musical sounds from this most intractable of instruments."

Alan Langford: Composed the Concertante for Harmonica and Strings.

Max Saunders (1908-2000): Composed the Sonatina for Harmonica and Piano.

5.5 Important Works by Globally Renowned Composers

Concerto for Harmonica and Orchestra (1953)

Arthur Benjamin (1893-1960) was an Australian-born composer (later settled in the UK), known for Jamaican Rumba.

His Concerto for Harmonica and Orchestra was composed in 1953, written for Larry Adler at the encouragement of Vaughan Williams, but it also became an important piece for Reilly.

The three movements of the piece are: I. Romanza (Allegro non troppo), II. Canzona Semplice (Andante poco lento), III. Rondo Amabile (Allegretto). It premiered on August 15, 1953, at the Proms concert in the Royal Albert Hall, London, with the London Symphony Orchestra (LSO) conducted by Basil Cameron. Tommy Reilly later recorded the work with the London Sinfonietta (Argo ZRG 905, 1979).

The ensemble includes double woodwinds, two trumpets, xylophone, celesta, and strings, reflecting Benjamin's pursuit of colorful orchestration.

Concerto for Harmonica and Orchestra (1955)

Heitor Villa-Lobos (1887-1959), Brazil's most important composer, was commissioned by another classical chromatic harmonica virtuoso of the last century, John Sebastian, to compose the Concerto for Harmonica and Orchestra. This is one of the few works in the harmonica repertoire written by a "world-class" master, and it stands alongside the Guitar Concerto by the Brazilian guitar virtuoso Segovia as one of Villa-Lobos' significant late contributions.

The composer sat at a huge semicircular desk, on which there was a pot of strong black coffee, several cigars, and many ashtrays, as he composed several pieces simultaneously, occasionally glancing at the television. He always wore a hat..." Obviously, John Sebastian was not quite satisfied with Villa's original cadenza, so they worked together to modify the passage:

The work has a three-movement structure: I. Allegro Moderato, II. Andante, III. Allegro. The second movement is particularly outstanding, "placing the soloist and orchestra against a rich, unexpected harmonic color background." Villa-Lobos fully utilizes the harmonica's expressive capabilities in the high register while exploring the instrument's harmonic and chromatic possibilities.

This piece was recorded by Reilly with the London Sinfonietta (conducted by David Atherton) and is one of the core tracks of the 1993 Chandos album.

Concerto for Harmonica and Orchestra (1962)

The American modernist composer Henry Cowell (1897-1965) composed the Concerto for Harmonica and Orchestra in 1962.

This piece was composed by Cowell at the request of the harmonica player John Sebastian. It was originally planned to be performed and recorded in Rome, but the plan was not realized due to Sebastian's sudden illness.

Premiere: The piece was premiered many years after Cowell's death, in 1986, by harmonica soloist Robert Bonfiglio with the Brooklyn Philharmonic Orchestra, conducted by Lukas Foss.

East-West Fusion: In his later years, Cowell showed a strong interest in non-Western music. This piece was deeply influenced by Japanese Gagaku, and Cowell attempted to simulate the sound of the traditional Japanese instrument "Shō" using the harmonica.

Movement Structure:

The piece is divided into three movements, with an overall style described as solemn, mournful, and richly colorful.

First Movement: Features a wave-like modal melody.

Second Movement: Begins with a long solo harmonica melody, accompanied by pizzicato strings.

Unique elements: In addition to the harmonica, the piece also features highly challenging parts for timpani and other percussion instruments.

Cowell introduced his signature "Tone Clusters" concept into this piece, utilizing the harmonica's ability to sound multiple reeds simultaneously, creating a sustained and non-percussive cluster harmony similar to the Japanese sho. Music critics believe that this work fully demonstrates the harmonica's potential in terms of melodic range and timbral variation, although in live performances, the balance between the solo harmonica and the large orchestra has always been a technical challenge.

5.6 Artistic Value and Historical Significance

Reilly successfully attracted top composers from Vaughan Williams, Villa-Lobos to Arnold, Benjamin to write for the harmonica. These composers were not "condescending" to do so, but rather discovered the unique tonal potential of the harmonica - its "vocal quality" and "extremely wide range of expression".

Reilly's commissioned works encompass a range of techniques from Baroque to modern styles, forming a complete teaching system from beginner (such as Old Scottish Air) to advanced (such as Spivakovsky's Concerto). These pieces continue to provide technical standards and artistic models for harmonica players today.

Chapter 6 The Birth of the Silver Mouth Organ: Instrument Innovation and Manufacturing Craftsmanship (1967)

6.1 The Opportunity for Collaboration with Hohner

Tommy Reilly's contributions to the art of the harmonica are not only reflected in his performances and commissioned works but also in the innovation of the instrument itself. In the early 1960s, Reilly was already a world-renowned harmonica player, but he was not entirely satisfied with the existing harmonicas. He believed that there was room for improvement in the manufacturing quality of harmonicas, especially for the performance of classical music.

Robert Farnon's composition Prelude and Dance was the direct trigger—it was so technically and musically demanding that Reilly concluded that the standard Hohner 270 was no longer sufficient (see Section 5.3). The Hohner 270 Super Chromonica was the standard instrument for professional players at the time, but its wooden comb was prone to swelling from moisture, affecting airtightness and intonation, and the standard reeds were inadequate when faced with the extreme dynamic range and tonal variation requirements of Farnon's piece.

Hohner 270 Super Chromonica Chromatic Harmonica

A deeper motivation was Reilly's long-held concept of the "silver harmonica." In the same interview, he explained: "I've always had the idea of a silver harmonica. Silver is the best material for me because it has already proven its worth on the flute." This cross-instrument material inspiration reflects Reilly's acoustic thinking: the density, resonance characteristics, and durability of silver make it the preferred material for high-end wind instruments, and the harmonica, as a wind instrument, should benefit from this tradition.

In 1967, Reilly initiated a collaboration with Hohner to develop the first silver reed chromatic harmonica—the Silver Concerto Chromonica.

6.2 Detailed Process of Chromatic Harmonica Development

Reilly's collaboration with Hohner was a long and complex process, involving a great deal of technological innovation.

Reilly detailed his requirements for the ideal harmonica: a warm and mellow tone, equal temperament tuning, high sensitivity response, excellent durability, and ergonomic comfort.

In terms of material selection, extensive experiments were conducted. Ultimately, they decided to use pure silver for the comb material. Silver's acoustic properties are superior to traditional brass and aluminum, capable of producing a warmer, rounder tone. The reed material was also specially treated to improve response speed and pitch stability.

In 1967, Reilly went to a silversmith in Covent Garden, London, to commission the prototype for the first silver chromatic harmonica. This location was chosen with symbolic intent: Covent Garden was the heart of London's classical music scene, and Reilly's choice to seek craftsmanship there indicated his intention to integrate harmonica making into the tradition of high-end instrument crafting.

However, after the harmonica was crafted, it was found impossible to assemble – the silver material differed from the engineering requirements of a standard harmonica, causing the reeds to not fit properly or the pitch to be unreliable.

The refinement stage after prototyping involved crucial technical collaboration. Reilly's student Douglas Tate—"at that time my student, who helped me perfect it with his technical skills"—participated in the subsequent engineering optimization. As an engineer, Tate utilized his technical knowledge to solve the assembly challenges of the silver harmonica, making the world's first silver harmonica a reality.

Douglas Tate later became a significant figure in the field of harmonica manufacturing, and this early collaboration marked the beginning of his career. The mentor-apprentice collaboration model ensured that the new instrument met Reilly's artistic vision while also being engineering feasible.

Douglas Tate (1934-2005)

Douglas Tate was one of Tommy Reilly's most important students and a key figure in the history of harmonica making. His relationship with Reilly was not only that of master and pupil but also engineering collaborators. (For Tate's biography, see section 6.5 of this chapter.)

The internal structure of the chromatic harmonica was redesigned to optimize the airflow channels. Reilly, based on his playing experience, proposed many improvements, such as enhancing the mechanical structure of the keys and increasing the elasticity of the springs to make the keys more responsive.

Pitch accuracy is one of the most important issues in classical music performance. Reilly insisted that the chromatic harmonica must be tuned to equal temperament, consistent with the pitch of the piano and the orchestra. Engineers conducted precise calculations and adjustments to the thickness and shape of the reeds, and Reilly personally participated in the testing of every note.

After the prototyping was completed, Reilly conducted extensive testing. He used the silver harmonica in various performances and recordings, thoroughly evaluating its performance. During the testing process, he found that the initial design had less than ideal tone in the high register, and suggested fine-tuning the airflow channels, ultimately resolving this issue.

6.3 Collaboration with Hohner to Release Silver Concerto

The business negotiations with Hohner were equally dramatic. When Reilly demonstrated the new instrument in Trossingen (Hohner's headquarters), the management was initially "hesitant" – "I had the feeling they didn't want to do it, but didn't want to say no." The turning point came when Reilly revealed that "Yamaha was also interested in purchasing the product license" – the competitive reaction of "Yamaha? Impossible!" prompted Hohner to change their stance, ultimately signing a contract that turned Reilly's development into a Hohner product, named the "Hohner Silver Concerto." This negotiation process revealed Reilly's business acumen: he was not merely an "artist," but a "cultural entrepreneur" who understood the value of intellectual property and could employ competitive strategies.

Hohner Silver Concerto Chromatic Harmonica

In 1968-1969, the chromatic harmonica was mass-produced under the name "Hohner Silver Concerto," featuring technical specifications that were very advanced for the time:

The Silver Concerto became the de facto standard concert harmonica over the years. The Hohner company made minor adjustments to this design, but its basic structure has persisted to this day.

It is worth mentioning that Reilly once complained that Hohner had changed the design of the slide button without his consent during mass production - "That could only be decided by someone who has never played the harmonica." This reflects his extreme attention to detail regarding the instrument.

The success of the silver harmonica proved the market demand for high-quality harmonicas. Subsequently, Hohner and other harmonica manufacturers began to introduce more high-quality harmonicas aimed at the classical music market. The twelve-tone equal temperament tuning used in the silver harmonica later became the standard for classical harmonicas.

Reilly's student Sigmund Groven recalled: "Tommy's contribution to the development of the harmonica was crucial. As a harmonica teacher, he was unparalleled anywhere; his tutorials and etudes are standard works, and in 1967, he created the world's first concert harmonica."

In his later years, Reilly tried the customized chromatic harmonica by Polle and declared it the finest harmonica he had ever played, even stating that "he would no longer play any other brand." This evaluation not only reflected his relentless pursuit of the highest quality in instruments but also indicated the continuous advancement in harmonica manufacturing technology.

6.4 The Rolls-Royce of Harmonicas - Polle Concert Harmonica

Tommy Reilly, one of the most influential classical harmonica players of the 20th century, witnessed the harmonica's arduous journey from a folk instrument to the concert stage. In 1967, when he first commissioned a silver chromatic harmonica in London, this act itself was an important statement about the status of the harmonica as a musical instrument.

This silver harmonica was mass-produced by Hohner according to Reilly's specifications (see 6.3), however, as Tommy Reilly regretfully pointed out, this pioneering product did not receive continuous technical iteration and optimization. Later, in a letter to Pollestad, Tommy Reilly hailed the Concert Harmonica he crafted as "the Rolls-Royce of all harmonicas," a statement that not only marked a shift in his personal choice of instrument but also symbolized the paradigm shift of the concert harmonica from an industrialized product to a handcrafted masterpiece.

From Silver Concerto to Concert Harmonica: A Technical Genealogy

For most of the 20th century, harmonica manufacturing consistently adhered to the logic of large-scale industrial production, emphasizing standardization and cost control rather than fine-tuning for the individual needs of professional players. Reilly's attempt in 1967, although breaking the material limitations, was still constrained by the industrial framework of the time in terms of mechanical structure, adaptability, and subsequent maintenance convenience.

This state of technical stagnation continued until the early 1980s. While serving as a resident teacher at the Norwegian Harmonica Seminar, Reilly met Georg Pollestad, who had a background in industrial design and mold-making. This encounter became a pivotal moment in the history of harmonica manufacturing. Pollestad not only understood Reilly's vision for the ideal concert harmonica but also possessed the technical ability to make it a reality.

This encounter changed the situation. As a professionally trained industrial designer and mold maker, Pollestad, after seeing Reilly's Hohner Silver Concerto in 1981, declared to the Norwegian harmonica player Sigmund Groven that he would make such an instrument himself. Unlike mass production on an industrial assembly line, Pollestad adopted a small-batch, handmade model, which allowed him to make immediate adjustments based on the player's specific feedback. The first custom-made harmonica completed a year later was highly praised by Groven and publicly showcased at a concert, marking the initial validation of Polle's production philosophy.

In 1990, Reilly revealed to fans after a concert in Munich that he had switched to "a better model made by a Norwegian named Georg Pollestad." This was not merely a brand change but signified a fundamental shift in harmonica manufacturing from industrial standards to handcrafted customization. By 1995, the Polle concert harmonica officially began accepting custom orders; in 2002, the Polle harmonica workshop was established in the village of Bryne, Norway, forming a stable production and development base.

What is the "Rolls-Royce" Standard

Reilly described the Polle harmonica as "the Rolls-Royce of all harmonicas" in his letter, a comparison that was not mere hyperbole but pointed to a series of specific and tangible technical specifications. Pollestad's philosophy of craftsmanship clearly distinguished itself from traditional harmonica manufacturing: "Traditional harmonica design makes compromises to optimize industrial production, whereas the Polle concept strictly prioritizes the performer's perspective, making no sacrifices in musical or technical quality."

Standard Edition Polle Concert Harmonica

In terms of material application, the Polle harmonica adopts aerospace-grade precision manufacturing standards. The standard model uses 925 sterling silver for the cover plates and the body, employing a unique hollow weight reduction technique - the silver block is cut in half, the excess silver inside is hollowed out, and then joined with precision screws - to keep the total weight of the harmonica at 214 grams, much lighter than the bulky feel of the early Silver Concerto. The sterling silver material not only gives the tone a warm quality but also has excellent thermal conductivity, allowing it to be quickly heated by hand warmth, avoiding the common cold feel of metal instruments.

Silver harmonica combs by Polle with openwork hollow technology

The innovation of the slide mechanism is the core of Reilly's special praise. The slide mechanism of traditional chromatic harmonicas often caused stuttering due to saliva accumulation, metal oxidation, or mechanical tolerances, which is the technical root of the "constant slide stuttering problem" mentioned in Reilly's letter. Pollestad's solution reflects his professional background as an industrial designer: the slide moves between two fixed slide rails, with the contact surfaces featuring precision longitudinal grooves, forming a self-lubricating mechanism; coupled with a dual shock absorber design, the slide action achieves a smooth and silent state. More crucially, the entire mouthpiece and slide system adopt a modular design, allowing for easy disassembly and maintenance, solving the professional pain point of difficult maintenance in traditional harmonicas.

The ergonomic design also reflects a deep understanding of the player's needs. On both sides of the mouthpiece, there are longitudinal grooves that form a saliva drainage system, collecting saliva to lubricate the contact surface between the lips and the mouthpiece, significantly reducing friction discomfort during long performances. The screws that secure the mouthpiece are made of titanium, a detail aimed at avoiding current interference caused by the contact of different metals, eliminating the faint electric shock sensation that players might feel. The LCD thermometer integrated on the side of the instrument provides scientific performance state monitoring, helping players to bring out the best of the instrument's performance under optimal temperature conditions.

From Sterling Silver to Titanium: The Technological Frontier

The technical evolution of Polle harmonicas did not remain at the level of the 1990s. Starting from 2009, Pollestad began systematically developing titanium versions to meet the professional players' demand for lighter weight. Titanium alloy, a high-end material in the aerospace industry, boasts an exceptionally high strength-to-weight ratio and excellent corrosion resistance, but its processing difficulty is far greater than that of traditional instrument materials. In recent years, the Polle workshop has even started making titanium reed plates in-house, significantly improving the instrument's responsiveness and tonal consistency by reducing the tolerance between the reeds and the plate.

Currently, Polle offers two choices for body materials: pure silver and titanium. The former continues the classic warm tone and texture, while the latter represents the ultimate in lightweight and modern design. This dual-track strategy both respects the silver tradition established in Reilly's era and aligns with the development trends of contemporary performance techniques.

Performance Verification and Historical Positioning

Tommy Reilly, as a recipient of the 1992 Member of the Order of the British Empire (MBE) - an honor that made him the only harmonica player to receive such recognition - his professional evaluation carries indisputable authority. In his letter, his triple endorsement of the Polle harmonica - "better appearance, far superior tone, and the push-button mechanism is simply a work of art" - actually established the criteria for judging high-end concert harmonicas: not only must they meet acoustic standards, but they must also achieve the same level of excellence in mechanical craftsmanship and visual aesthetics.

Reilly's declaration "from now on, I will only play Polle harmonicas" marked a decisive turning point, signaling a subtle shift in the power dynamics of the harmonica manufacturing industry. Traditionally, players had to adapt to the standards set by industrial manufacturers; in the Polle model, however, the instrument maker achieved a true fit between the instrument and the player by deeply understanding the player's physical experience and technical needs. The long-term use of Polle harmonicas by world-class players such as Sigmund Groven further validated the professional sustainability of this manufacturing model.

From a historical perspective, the Polle Concert Harmonica represents the third generation of high-end custom instruments in the evolution of harmonica manufacturing. The first generation was the Silver Concerto commissioned by Reilly in 1967, which first introduced precious metals into harmonica making; the second generation was the CBH 2012 designed by Chamber Huang in 1973, which attempted a mix of resin and pure silver but failed to become a practical standard due to its weight of 1275 grams; the third generation is the Polle Concert Harmonica, which, under Reilly's direct guidance, solved the weight and mechanical issues of the previous generation and established the gold standard for modern concert harmonicas through a handcrafted custom model.

The "Rolls-Royce" analogy in the letter was later widely cited in the industry, becoming synonymous with the Polle harmonica. This letter witnessed the perfect fusion of industrial design and musical art: a non-playing technician, through a deep understanding of the player's physical experience and technical vision, created a professional instrument that surpassed industrial standards.

The Polle Harmonica Workshop continues to uphold the tradition of small-batch, handcrafted harmonicas in the village of Bryne, Norway, with each harmonica embodying the philosophy passed down from Reilly's era – resisting industrialization, serving only the quality of music. In this sense, Reilly's letter from the early 1990s not only documented a personal choice of instrument but also foreshadowed the future direction of harmonica manufacturing: a return from standardized production to personalized customization, an elevation from mass manufacturing to handcrafted art.

Currently, the Polle Concert Harmonica is only available for pre-order production until 2024. It is planned to produce the last batch of instruments in 2025, after which the production of the Polle Concert Harmonica will cease.

6.5.1 Starting Point of the Engineer

Douglas William Richard Tate was a Yorkshireman, born in 1934 in Yarm-on-Tees, a town on the River Tees in northeast England, where his father was a chief engineer at the ICI ammonia plant in Billingham. After leaving school at 18, he moved to London to become an apprentice, and thanks to his outstanding engineering talent, he rose through the ranks to become the research and design director of ICL (International Computers Limited), Europe's largest computer company - dot matrix printers, card readers, constant current welding machines for the automotive industry, and teaching machines designed for schools and the World Health Organization were all his creations.

His connection with the harmonica began at age 6: a school notice board displayed a rule - "Mouthorgans Forbidden". This ban instead ignited his curiosity, but like most children, his interest only lasted three weeks. It was not until he graduated from high school and started working in a factory that he picked up the harmonica again, playing it during work; the ridicule of his colleagues sparked his competitive spirit, and he was determined to "conquer this instrument".

Two years later, in 1955, he entered the All-Britain Harmonica Championships for the first time and placed fourth. The biggest revelation from this competition for him was: to play the harmonica well, one must first learn to read sheet music. So he began self-studying music theory, progressing from fifth place nationally in 1956 and second at the Wembley Music Festival, to winning Wembley and placing fourth in all of Britain in 1957, earning him a spot in the 1957 Luxembourg World Championships—his first overseas competition. He went on to compete in Paris 1958, Innsbruck 1959, Barcelona 1960, Strasbourg 1963, Delft 1965, and culminating in the pinnacle event in Karlsruhe 1967.

Interestingly, this future world champion initially couldn't read music either. The 1958 archives of Harmonica News record that his "only formal lessons" were a few one or two-hour sessions with Tommy Reilly: "The most important thing about taking lessons from Tommy Reilly was to listen and watch him play - you watched and learned how those effects were produced." To refine his tone, he also studied Reilly's throat vibrato and Larry Adler's tongue vibrato. He was also an expert on the Harmonetta (chord harmonica), having represented Britain in the 1957 World Championship's harmonica ensemble category with his own composition as a cadenza.

6.5.2 World Champion and Stainless Steel 270

In 1967, Douglas was determined to win the "Blue Ribbon" level chromatic harmonica category at the World Harmonica Championships - the previous champions in this category were Willi Burger in 1955 and Franz Chmel in 1963. He prepared like an engineer: he went back to take lessons from Reilly, and meticulously studied the winning strategies of past champions.

In this year, the prototype silver harmonica commissioned by Reilly from a London silversmith ran into problems—the silver body couldn't be properly assembled. Reilly turned to this engineering student: "At that time, my student, Douglas Tate, used his technical skills to help me perfect it." Using this experience and his own design ideas, Douglas, just days before the 1967 World Championship, crafted for himself a 270 modified harmonica with a stainless steel body (stainless steel comb, self-made cold-drawn reeds). On June 24, 1967, in Karlsruhe, Germany, he performed James Moody's Impromptu as the set piece and Larry Adler's arrangement of Enescu's Romanian Rhapsody No. 1 as the free piece, defeating contestants from 14 member countries to become the first world harmonica champion in British history (with his wife Janet as piano accompanist).

His "engineer's cunning" didn't stop there: in his early years, he competed in the 10-hole harmonica category using a chromatic harmonica with the slide removed, a loophole that was quickly closed by the competition organizers; his victories in the 10-hole category in Innsbruck and Barcelona were the first wins by a British player in world competitions.

6.5.3 Teacher, Musician, and "Harmonica Doctor"

After winning the championship, Douglas continued to work at ICL. In 1970, he decided to change careers and underwent teacher training in Luton, majoring in music; he then became the head of the music department at Dunstable and Wentworth Upper School, founding numerous music clubs. In 1984, he moved to a position as the computer coordinator in the business studies department of a secondary school, where he remained until his retirement in 1996. His experience in computer education led him to co-author "The Complete BBC Computer User Handbook" - a BBC computer that had entered most schools and homes in the UK.

As a performer, he played 250 shows at the Windmill Theatre in London at the end of 1961 with his duo "Tate Brothers" (sandwiched between two comedy dance acts); he played for the BBC through an introduction by the Hertfordshire County Music Director, provided theme music for TV shows, and collaborated with composer Phyllis Tate (no relation) to premiere a piece for harmonica, string quartet, piano, and tenor at the Cardiff Music Festival - it was his first time playing a piece where the "harmonica was on completely equal footing with the other instruments." He also studied Baroque performance practice with a local string quartet, an experience that "completely transformed his harmonica playing technique and musical outlook"; he worked with the London Philharmonic, London Symphony, and Halle orchestras, provided music for BBC programs like "Last of the Summer Wine," recorded with Rod Argent and Janet Edwards as the "Electrio," and even worked in the Beatles' recording studio.

But his most legendary identity was as the "Harmonica Doctor." Reilly tuned the first silver harmonica for him; Larry Adler also frequently sent his harmonicas to him for tuning. His key areas of improvement included: the seal and travel length of the slide, the shape of the airway at the top of the reed plate – his original "Tate Ramps" involved filling the airway with fiberglass resin and then grinding it into a ramp shape, which significantly improved the response; he insisted on polishing the reeds to eliminate stress points and extend their lifespan. He played his own modified harmonica – a stainless steel 270 model – for over 30 years without needing repairs; on his deathbed, he instructed Pat Missin – the only reed expert he trusted – to check this harmonica, and it was found that only one reed needed minor adjustment, and he specifically instructed not to dismantle the reed plate: "Any attached material might contribute to stability."

6.5.4 Renaissance: The Final Masterpiece

In the mid-1990s, Douglas connected with American designer Bobbie Giordano and patron Bill Henderson through the internet (an early user of the HARP-L mailing list, active since 1995). He poured all his years of verified modification ideas for the 270 into the collaboration with Giordano to design the Renaissance chromatic harmonica: smooth silver-plated covers without screws, stainless steel reed plates, and a short-travel, high-airtightness slide assembly. The first prototype debuted at the 1996 SPAH convention, and the first batch of production models hit the market in August 1997. Despite the high price, it was an immediate success, with over 50 pieces made, and was considered by many players to be the finest chromatic harmonica of its time.

In 2000, he was invited to become the president of the SPAH (Society for the Preservation and Advancement of the Harmonica) in the United States, steering the struggling organization back on track – he invited world-class artists such as Brendan Power, Fata Morgana, Philip Achille, Donald Black, Adler Trio, and Yasuhiro Fukuda to attend the annual conference, resolving internal conflicts between different generations and styles, and was re-elected for two terms. In December 2004, he was invited to participate in the Singapore Youth Harmonica Festival: he conducted workshops on instrument design and performance techniques, gave television and radio interviews, held masterclasses, and performed a duet with his good friend Yasuhiro Fukuda at the concert – the two humorously opened their performance by "pretending to adjust the tuning knobs," amusing the entire audience. The National University of Singapore offered him a one-year residency, but unfortunately, he was unable to accept the invitation.

In early 2005, he was diagnosed with brain cancer and passed away that same year. He left behind two books, Play The Harmonica Well and Make Your Harmonica Play Better; his collaboration with Seydel on the Renaissance mass production, which was continued by his family after his death, with the first Seydel models released in 2006. His widow, Barbara Tate, carried on his work: continuing to compile and publish Tommy Reilly's teaching materials (including The Tommy Reilly Harmonica Course with CD) and overseeing the digital restoration of Douglas's entire recording catalog (douglastate.com).

His world championship trophy was once seen by Ivan Richards being "used as a doorstop" — this engineer, who treated the world championship title as an ordinary thing, devoted his life's passion to "making the harmonica easier to play and stay in tune longer."

Chapter 7 Passing the Torch: Harmonica Education and Legacy

7.1 Core of Teaching: Technical Training with Music Expression as the Core

Tommy Reilly's life was not only that of a performer and composer but also that of a great music educator. Over several decades, he developed a complete, scientific, and systematic chromatic harmonica teaching system, laying the most solid foundation for the inheritance and development of harmonica art. The core of this teaching system is "technique training with musical expression as its core," which is in line with his performance philosophy that "technique always serves music."

Before Tommy Reilly, chromatic harmonica teaching was in a rudimentary "folk apprenticeship" stage, lacking a unified teaching system and scientific training methods. The vast majority of instruction focused solely on technical skills, neglecting the cultivation of musical literacy and the enhancement of musical expression. Many harmonica learners could perform difficult techniques but were unable to read full scores, understand harmony, comprehend the musical content of pieces, or deliver music with warmth and depth.

Tommy Reilly's teaching system completely revolutionized this traditional teaching model. He drew from the teaching systems of classical music institutions and combined them with the characteristics of the harmonica, creating a complete teaching system that "simultaneously enhances technical training and musical literacy, with skill mastery and musical expression complementing each other."

This teaching system is primarily divided into four levels, progressing step by step, and runs through the entire process of harmonica learning:

Basic Skills Layer: Focus on training the four core skills of breath control, single-note purity, pitch accuracy, and key precision. At this stage, he abandoned the traditional teaching method of "quickly playing melodies" and required learners to start with long tones and slow scales, first building a solid foundation and developing correct playing habits. Simultaneously, basic music theory and ear training were incorporated, allowing learners to establish the concept of "music before technique" from the beginning.

Technique Advancement Stage: Systematically teaching advanced techniques such as vibrato, glissando, tremolo, fast scales, legato, and tonguing. At this stage, his teaching is never just about demonstrating techniques; instead, he first explains to the learners the musical purpose of each technique, in what kind of musical context it should be used, and then proceeds to teach the technical points. He always emphasizes, "Only when you know what a technique is used to express can you truly master it."

Musical Interpretation Layer: This is the core of the teaching system. At this stage, he teaches learners how to analyze the structure, harmony, and emotional core of a piece, how to phrase, manage dynamics, and adjust timbre changes, and how to achieve a second creation of the piece and personal expression. His teaching is never about having students imitate his playing, but rather guiding them to find their own understanding of the piece and develop their own playing style.

Artistic Aesthetic Level: The Pinnacle of the Teaching System

He enhances students' musical appreciation and artistic quality comprehensively through classical music history, composers' biographies, and the analysis of different musical styles. He believes that how far a performer can ultimately go is not determined by how superb their technique is, but by how high their artistic appreciation is. He hopes that his students will not only be excellent harmonica players but also well-rounded musicians.

As early as the 1950s, Tommy Reilly began creating harmonica teaching materials. At that time, with the spread of his performances and recordings, more and more people started learning the harmonica, but there was no systematic, professional teaching material. Facing the scarcity of harmonica teaching resources and the confusion of playing standards, he spent decades combining his playing experience and teaching philosophy to create a series of complete harmonica teaching materials to solve this problem. Reilly founded the Tommy Reilly International Club (TRIC) from 1967 to 1971 and during this period wrote and published systematic teaching materials. These materials not only documented his technical system but also reflected his educational ideal of "elevating the harmonica to the same artistic status as string and woodwind instruments."

The Tommy Reilly Harmonica Course

The Tommy Reilly Harmonica Course, his first systematic harmonica tutorial from beginner to advanced levels, is also the first professional textbook in the history of chromatic harmonica that aligns with the classical music teaching system. This textbook fully follows his teaching methodology, progressing from basic music theory, playing posture, and breathing techniques to advanced playing techniques and musical interpretation. Each chapter is paired with targeted exercises and pieces, allowing learners to gradually master the core skills of harmonica playing while simultaneously enhancing their musical literacy.

This textbook, once published, quickly gained global popularity and was translated into dozens of languages, becoming a required study for harmonica learners worldwide. Even more than half a century after its publication, this textbook remains the most authoritative and widely used in the field of chromatic harmonica instruction. Study No.1-10 (Basic Single Notes): The core objective is to solve the most fundamental technical challenge of the chromatic harmonica - the purity of single notes. Reilly requires performers to maintain the same rounded tone in holes 8-12 (high register) as in holes 1-4 (low register), which necessitates precise control of oral cavity shape ('O' shape mouth) and breath pressure regulation.

Study No.90 Serenade: This is the iconic piece of the book, with a recording of Reilly's own performance preserved. The technical challenges include: breath control for long musical phrases (requiring the use of nose inhalation and mouth blowing techniques), tone control to imitate the vibrato of a violin, smooth transitions between slide and breath changes, and multi-hole embouchure and multi-chord switching while playing.

This book laid the foundation for future harmonica pedagogy.

First, technical standardization. In the 1950s, harmonica playing was generally characterized by "folk habits" (such as excessive reliance on vibrato, neglect of intonation). Reilly established pitch and timbre standards similar to string instruments through this set of etudes.

Secondly, the classicization of repertoire. By arranging works by composers such as Bach and Scarlatti, Reilly created the initial "standard repertoire" for the chromatic harmonica, which is still used by concert performers today.

Third, the education became templated. The book established a progressive teaching path of "single notes → scales → arpeggios → pieces," influencing the logic behind the writing of all subsequent chromatic harmonica teaching materials, including James Moody's Little Suite, Gordon Jacob's Five Pieces, and others.

7.3 Teaching Legend: TRIC International Harmonica Club

The birth of TRIC originated from a wife's distress.

After Tommy Reilly became famous, letters from music fans around the world poured in like snowflakes. As his wife, Ena Reilly took on the task of replying to all the letters. When technical questions arose, she had to wait until Tommy’s performance breaks to consult him before responding. This work gradually took up a lot of her time. Around 1965, Ena came up with a revolutionary idea: instead of replying to hundreds of letters separately, why not establish a club where harmonica enthusiasts could come to their home, learn directly from Tommy, and exchange ideas with each other?

This concept gave birth to TRIC - not only a teaching venue but also a testing ground for new works and a communication center for musicians and composers.

From Glory to Disbandment: 1967-1971

"I truly envy those who are fortunate enough to be members of TRIC. This is truly a great milestone in the history of the harmonica!" said Singaporean harmonica player Tay Teow Ming in an article published in the 2013 issue of the magazine Harmonica World.

During the four years from 1967 to 1971, TRIC established the chromatic harmonica as a serious classical instrument by setting teaching standards and creating an international elite education model. Its influence continues to this day through Reilly's student network, making it an unrepeatable legend in the history of harmonica education.

In the summer of 1970, the Norwegian Broadcasting Corporation (NRK) brought a film crew to Hamonds Farm to shoot a special program about Tommy's teaching activities at TRIC. This film can be viewed on the NRK website. It features Norwegian voice-over but lacks a credits list.

Ultimately, TRIC disbanded in 1971. This was a demanding period for both Ena and Tommy, and they had no financial sponsorship: membership fees were heavily subsidized, and the cost of staying at Hammonds Wood for a week was not even enough to cover meals; juggling TRIC's activities with Tommy's busy touring schedule and other work commitments was no easy feat—1971 was the year Tommy turned 52, at the peak of his skill, musicality, and fame, and he needed to earn more income for his family, making it an obvious choice to increase performance opportunities.

The original intention of the TRIC project was not for profit. In addition to charging low tuition fees, Tommy was very friendly to his students and would sometimes even give money back to those who were penniless (or looked it), letting them buy a much-needed pair of shoes. He saw TRIC more as a gathering place: to meet like-minded friends, drink tea, enjoy good food, laugh heartily, and incidentally teach some harmonica lessons; James Moody also got to try out new solo and ensemble pieces here. This world-class virtuoso charged only a few pounds to impart his secrets, so it is no wonder that TRIC gradually declined after just a few years. Even after closing TRIC, Tommy did not stop taking on students: he would continue to recruit a select few during breaks in his performances, especially in the summer, and often held workshops during harmonica festivals, benefiting many players.

According to archival records, active members of TRIC included: from the UK, Carol Bloxsome (later Axford), Graham Butcher, Bryan Mapperley, Brian Chaplin, Frank Semus, Johnny Stafford, Wally Chapman; from Ireland, John Ferguson; from Costa Rica, Dimas Barrantes; from Singapore, Ho Chong Wing; from Japan, Joe Sakimoto; from Hong Kong, Ho Kit Fun; from Germany, Doris Ehmann, Brigitte Burgbacher, Helmut Herold, Heiner Schwenkglenks; from Switzerland, Ernst Hotan; from the Netherlands, Peter Jansen; and Tariq Hameed, of Pakistani descent but based in the UK. Additionally, American harmonica professionals Richard Hayman and Jerry Murad were also members. In 1970, members premiered several new harmonica ensemble pieces by James Moody at the "Accordion and Harmonica Day" in Leicester, with performers including Reilly, James Hughes, Sigmund Groven, Brian Chaplin, Carol Bloxsome, Graham Butcher, and Bryan Mapperley.

7.4 In-Depth Analysis of Teaching Philosophy and Methods

Reilly's teaching philosophy was deeply influenced by his early violin studies and his collaboration with Spivakovsky. He emphasized the following aspects:

A Step-by-Step Teaching Method

Reilly's teaching emphasizes a step-by-step approach. His textbook The Tommy Reilly Harmonica Course is written based on this principle, starting from the most basic harmonica holding posture and breathing methods, and gradually introducing various playing techniques.

He particularly emphasized the importance of slow practice: "Do not play at a speed faster than you feel you can control until you are sure you can accurately master the correct articulation and dynamics without mistakes. We do this to avoid the slightest inaccuracy becoming permanent."

Emphasis on Musicality and Singability

Reilly's teaching was not just about technical training, but focused more on cultivating musicality. He believed that technique was merely a means, and music was the end goal.

He often encouraged students to "sing through the harmonica": "Imagine how a singer would perform it. Singing can help you understand how to shape the musical phrases." He believed that singing was the best way to understand musical expression, and through singing, students could naturally feel the phrasing, climax, and conclusion of a piece.

The Importance of Listening

Reilly placed great emphasis on the training of listening. He said, "You might be surprised when I tell you that listening to your own playing is the most important part of developing good playing technique. This applies to the harmonica and to all other instruments as well."

He taught his students to listen critically to their own playing, paying attention to every nuance, including duration, timbre, articulation, dynamics, speed, and phrasing. He said, "If you constantly focus on critically listening to yourself, you will no longer rush through certain passages. Instinctively, you will improve your movements and breathing techniques."

Stage Performance and Performance Psychology

Reilly also paid great attention to stage presence. His wife Ena, a professionally trained dancer and singer, taught him how to stand and move on stage. He instructed his students: stand steadily, feet apart at shoulder width; keep the body relaxed; hold the head straight; let the hand movements be natural.

Regarding performance psychology, Reilly believes that thorough preparation is the best way to overcome nervousness. He suggests replacing negative worries with positive thoughts, doing deep breathing before performing to help relax, and focusing attention on the music rather than the audience's reaction.

7.5 Tommy Reilly: Two Great Successors from East and West

Tommy Reilly's teaching legacy is most vividly embodied in the numerous world-class harmonica virtuosos he nurtured. These students not only inherited his playing system and artistic philosophy but also promoted the art of the harmonica globally, spreading his musical spark to every corner of the world. Among them, the most representative core disciples are the Norwegian harmonica master Sigmund Groven and the Japanese harmonica master Yasuo Watani.

Sigmund Groven: The Most Orthodox Artistic Inheritor

Sigmund Groven, a national treasure of Norway as a harmonica player, was also Tommy Reilly's most prized student, hailed as "the most authentic inheritor of Tommy Reilly's artistic philosophy."

Sigmund Groven was born into a musical family in Norway. As a teenager, he was deeply moved upon hearing Tommy Reilly's records for the first time and was determined to become a classical harmonica player like him. At the age of 16, Sigmund Groven traveled to England to become a student of Tommy Reilly, beginning a decades-long journey of learning and collaboration.

Under Tommy Reilly's meticulous tutelage, Sigmund Groven not only fully mastered his playing system and technical skills but also deeply understood his artistic philosophy of "technique serving music." Tommy Reilly taught him not only playing techniques but also how to understand music, interpret music, and how to become a well-rounded musician. Under the influence of his teacher, Sigmund Groven has always been committed to the performance, composition, and promotion of classical harmonica, following in his teacher's footsteps to continue advancing the harmonica's development in the field of classical music.

Sigmund Groven did not let his teacher down. He became the first harmonica player in history to hold a solo concert at Carnegie Hall, collaborated with dozens of top orchestras around the world, composed a large number of original harmonica works, and arranged countless classical pieces, bringing classical harmonica art to a wider recognition in Europe and globally. He has often stated in interviews, "Without Tommy Reilly, there would be no me today. He was not only my teacher but also my lifelong spiritual mentor. Everything I have done is to continue his unfinished work, to give the harmonica a more important place in the classical music hall."

Yasuo Watani: The Eastern Legacy and Promotion

Yasuo Watani is a top classical harmonica player in Japan, a significant disciple of Tommy Reilly, and the key figure in spreading Tommy Reilly's playing system and artistic philosophy to Asia.

When Yasuo Watani was young, he traveled to the UK to study and became a student of Tommy Reilly, learning classical harmonica performance. During his studies, he was deeply impressed by Tommy Reilly's performance skills and artistic philosophy, not only systematically learning his performance system but also deeply researching his teaching methods. After completing his studies and returning to Japan, Yasuo Watani promoted Tommy Reilly's performance system and teaching philosophy in Asian countries such as Japan, Korea, and China, cultivating a large number of harmonica talents in Asia, allowing classical harmonica art to take root and flourish in Asia.

At the same time, Yasuo Watani has held numerous concerts dedicated to Tommy Reilly's works, recorded a complete collection of Tommy Reilly's works, and written extensive research papers and teaching materials on Tommy Reilly's performance art, allowing harmonica enthusiasts in Asia to comprehensively and deeply understand Tommy Reilly's artistic achievements. He once said, "Tommy Reilly is the founder of the chromatic harmonica; he paved the way for us. Our responsibility is to make this path wider and longer, so that more people in Asia can recognize the artistic charm of the harmonica as an instrument."

In addition to Sigmund Groven and Yasuo Watani, Tommy Reilly has students and followers all over the world. These musicians, some of whom have become professional soloists, music educators, harmonica designers, and composers, are all continuing Tommy Reilly's artistic philosophy in their respective fields, advancing the development of harmonica art, and ensuring that his legacy is passed down through the generations. Reilly's students are spread across the globe, including Germany, the Netherlands, Japan, the United States, and more. These students have spread Reilly's teaching philosophy and performance techniques to all corners of the world.

Chen Guanbin's Recollection of Attending Master Classes

He had a habit: every time he met a harmonica master, he would ask the same question: "How do you produce your vibrato?"

The question seems simple, yet it touches upon the most core technical secrets of harmonica playing. However, the answers received are usually unsatisfactory, because these masters are unable to tell a shortcut that can be learned instantly. Some say it is "natural," others say it is "developed through practice," but none provide specific technical guidance.

Compared to other harmonica players, Tommy Reilly's tone is clearly much richer and more nuanced. His sound can be as warm as velvet or as sharp as a bell; it can imitate the sustained notes of string instruments or capture the crispness of woodwinds. This versatility is rare in the harmonica world.

In 1998, at the Dartington Music Summer School, Guanbin Chen finally had the opportunity to ask Reilly this question that had troubled him for years. He seized the moment and asked, "How do you produce such rich and varied tone colours?"

According to Chen Guanbin's description, the scene was highly visual: he saw Reilly gazing into the distance, exhaling "Imagination!" meditatively and slowly, ending with that one word. This single word—"Imagination"—made Chen Guanbin ponder for 20 years, experiment for 20 years, yet he never found a completely satisfying answer.

In Uwe Warschkow's later published book Harmonica Lessons with Tommy Reilly, there are entire 4 pages dedicated to discussing "Tone Colours", which shows that not only Chen Guanbin, but all of Reilly's students and followers had the same question.

The Musician's Daily Life Beyond the Classroom

Over the twenty years of lessons, besides the technical aspects, there were also numerous precious everyday moments: when visiting James Moody, the old partner would play the grand piano to showcase his new work - Sonatina - "I was mainly thinking of those ambitious young players, there is such a lack of original chamber music for the harmonica"; Ena's tea was something Uwe would always reminisce about ("the activities and duties of the day were merely a bridge to the next tea break"); one night a tornado swept across the estate, leaving six century-old oak trees blocking the driveway, and that afternoon Tommy still had to rush to rehearse with the Royal Philharmonic Orchestra; and then there was his recording studio philosophy - "a studio musician must be an excellent sight-reader, studio time is expensive, there is no time for multiple takes."

7.7 Tommy Reilly's Technical Secrets on Tone Quality

Tommy Reilly's mastery of the chromatic harmonica tone was truly extraordinary, and Mr. Chen Guanbin found a 1979 radio interview recording of Reilly. In this precious recording, Reilly demonstrates in detail the changes in harmonica tone. Even if your English listening skills are not great, just listening to the first 3 minutes of his demonstration on the changes in harmonica tone will allow you to understand his technical points. This is the only detailed revelation and explanation of Tommy Reilly's chromatic harmonica tone.

The truth is, he repeatedly mentioned this in his teaching materials. It is a systematic technical framework involving the precise control of four variables:

Warm Tone Formula:

Hold the harmonica deeper (while keeping the throat open) + Seal the palm (completely closed) = Warm tone

Bright/Penetrating Tone Formula:

Hold the harmonica slightly shallower (while applying more pressure with your lips) + open your palm (slightly) = strong tone

Reilly explains that these four elements can be combined to create infinite variations:

The Four Elements Formula of Timbre Variation

Air pressure dynamics

  • Mouthpiece depth / Embouchure aperture

  • Lip muscle control

  • Hand cupping resonance chamber

→ can be transformed into countless tonal variations

After Chen Guanbin understood this principle, he exclaimed:

The principle is just that simple. Then, after another 20 years of diligent and tireless practice, (perhaps, probably, maybe) you will be able to learn it.

This sentence not only expresses the hardships of technical learning but also implies the profound skill of Reilly—simple principles require decades of honing to truly master.

In Uwe Warschkow's book, another layer of Reilly's tone philosophy is also explored: tone is not merely a technical issue but also involves the player's physical structure as well as their psychological and mental state.

Reilly once told Uwe that he thought Uwe was a calm and serious person, so he would unconsciously pursue the so-called "perfect" sound. However, Reilly emphasized:

"But music is not notes, music is feelings."

Since emotions encompass joy, anger, sorrow, and happiness, they should not be expressed through just a single so-called perfect sound. Reilly believes that in certain situations, a perfect tone can be inappropriate - in a sad musical passage, the tone might need some "roughness" or "cracks" to convey the emotion.

This philosophy deeply influenced Chen Guanbin's understanding. He later wrote: So if one cannot produce the rich tonal variations like Reilly, at least one can comfort oneself by being a "simple and upright" person.—This statement is both self-deprecating and an insight into Reilly's philosophy: everyone's character determines their tone, and it cannot be forced.

When discussing the tone of different harmonicas, Singaporean friend Cheng Jang Ming (the only harmonica player with over a million views on YouTube for classical chromatic harmonica performances) also mentioned a similar principle: everyone subconsciously has a certain preconceived idea of what a harmonica should sound like, so whether you play a Polle silver harmonica or an Easttop, you will develop towards the sound in your heart's desire, and ultimately they will all sound the same. This means that the instrument is just a tool, and the true tone comes from the player's inner imagination - which coincides with Reilly's concept of "Imagination".

Tommy Reilly not only had a rich variety of tones, but he was also adept at imitating the playing of other harmonica players, demonstrating his absolute mastery of tone control.

Once, Uwe Warschkow was listening to Reilly's past performance recordings at Reilly's home, when he came across a track of Jerry Murad's Harmonicats' "Peg O My Heart". This left him quite puzzled: why would Reilly play this famous trio piece?

Reilly explained with a smile that Jerry Murad was his good friend, and he had listened to most of the Harmonicats' records. At that time, there was a TV show that wanted him to record a piece, and the producer was particularly keen on "Peg O My Heart" (this was the Harmonicats' 1947 mega-hit that set a Guinness World Record), and they even found an accordionist to accompany him.

The producer demanded that Reilly play exactly like Murad. Reilly, with his deep understanding of Murad's playing style, flawlessly imitated Murad's tone and phrasing.

Later, Murad visited Reilly at his home, and Reilly specially gave this tape to Murad. After listening, Murad laughed and cursed on the spot:

"You bastard!"

How accurately he could imitate is no longer verifiable, but this demonstrated Reilly's absolute control over tone - he could not only create his own tone, but also precisely replicate the tonal characteristics of others.

Tone Training in the Classroom: Gradation and Vibrato System

Uwe's recordings also documented Reilly's tone training method: starting from the hardest, most piercing fortissimo long note, gradually transitioning to the softest, most rounded pianissimo long note without interrupting the airflow, then reversing the process, all while maintaining stable intonation - this exercise integrates three variables: the opening and closing of both hands (resonance chamber), the state of the lips and throat, and breath pressure. As for vibrato, it was divided by register: "I only use throat vibrato in the low register below the sixth hole, and only hand vibrato in the high register above the seventh hole" - the reeds in the high register are extremely small, and throat vibrato would cause intonation to waver; hand vibrato must never fully close the hands, "as that would produce a wah-wah sound, making the intonation flat." Vibrato must align with the rhythm and the string section, "vibrato is a seasoning, not the main dish."

7.8 Globalization of Harmonica Education: From College Courses to International Dissemination

Tommy Reilly's contributions to harmonica education extend beyond nurturing a group of top-tier performers; they encompass his pivotal role in advancing the globalization and professionalization of harmonica education. This has led to the harmonica being introduced into the classrooms of professional music institutions for the first time, establishing it as a formal discipline within music education.

Before Tommy Reilly, there were no professional music institutions anywhere in the world offering harmonica as a major. The harmonica was regarded as a folk amateur instrument, completely unqualified to enter the professional music education system. However, Tommy Reilly, through his lifelong efforts, completely changed this situation.

As early as the 1960s, Tommy Reilly began collaborating with top British music institutions such as the Royal Academy of Music and the Royal Northern College of Music, offering harmonica masterclasses and elective courses. This allowed students in professional music schools to systematically encounter classical harmonica playing for the first time. Through his performance prowess and teaching achievements, he demonstrated to the classical music education community that the harmonica is a serious instrument worthy of inclusion in the professional music education system.

Driven by his continuous efforts, in the 1970s, the Royal Academy of Music officially incorporated the chromatic harmonica into its formal curriculum, establishing undergraduate and master's degree programs for harmonica performance. This was the first time a top music institution had established a harmonica major in global music history. This event was a milestone in the development of harmonica art, marking the formal recognition of the harmonica as a legitimate instrument in professional classical music education.

Subsequently, driven by his students and followers, dozens of top music institutions around the world, including the Norwegian Academy of Music, the Hanover University of Music, Drama and Media in Germany, the Tokyo University of the Arts in Japan, and the Central Conservatory of Music in China, successively established harmonica courses and harmonica degree programs. Harmonica education officially entered a stage of globalization and professionalization.

In addition, Tommy Reilly promoted harmonica education globally through international harmonica festivals, masterclasses, and competitions. He served as the artistic director and judge for numerous World Harmonica Festivals, conducting masterclasses where he imparted his playing techniques and teaching philosophies to harmonica enthusiasts from around the world. He also spearheaded the establishment of several international harmonica competitions and awards, encouraging the growth of young harmonica players and nurturing a wealth of talent for the development of harmonica art.

To this day, harmonica programs at professional music institutions worldwide continue to use the textbooks he created and follow the teaching system he established. With a lifetime of dedication, he built a comprehensive framework for harmonica education, transforming the harmonica from a folk pastime into a professional, systematic musical discipline. This is one of the greatest legacies he left to the art of the harmonica.

The Tommy Reilly Harmonica Course was digitally republished in 2006 by Barbara Tate (widow of Douglas Tate) and reissued in 2021 by Norsk Musikforlag in book + 2CD format, proving its enduring pedagogical value.

British Harmonica Organization: A Supplementary Note

7.9 Seventeenth Lesson Transcript Highlights: Uwe Warschkow's Class Notes

German accountant Uwe Warschkow traveled to Hammonds Wood annually from 1978 until the master's passing in 2000, studying with Reilly for a week each year, a period spanning over two decades. He documented this experience in "Harmonica Lessons with Tommy Reilly," using Gordon Jacob's "Five Pieces" as the textbook, meticulously recording Reilly's teaching process lesson by lesson. Below is a distillation of the core content from these records.

First Principle: Keep the Head Stable, Move the Instrument

The first lesson began with "Lullaby," and Reilly first corrected the "most fundamental mistake"—moving the head to align with the holes:

"Your head must always remain stable, with only the harmonica moving. Otherwise, you will never have absolute control over your playing, nor will you ever achieve perfect legato."

He broke this principle down into a set of actionable systems:

  • Shortest Path: When moving from one hole to another, always take the shortest straight line and use the smallest possible movement. When playing adjacent holes, you can shorten the moving distance by slightly tilting the instrument; when making large jumps, the harmonica must be kept level.

  • Lips Never Leaving the Harmonica: Even for octave or two-octave jumps, the lips should stay glued to the harmonica and slide across, completing the movement while the previous note is still sounding — "You should not see any movement on your lips."

  • Tongue Guidance: The sensation of the tongue tip controls the sliding position on the mouthpiece.

  • Holding the Harmonica Like Wearing a Kid Glove: The thumb is on the bottom cover plate, the index finger is on the top cover plate, hold it gently without squeezing; the left arm is completely relaxed like a support, movement is driven by the right wrist. "Any tension will affect your playing."

  • Mirror Practice: Check in the mirror every day to ensure the head is not moving and that there are no unnecessary movements.

Green Book Exercise 1: The entire piece consists of sixteenth notes on adjacent holes, specifically designed for legato and hand-slide training

Legato: The Soul of the Harmonica

Legato holds the utmost importance in Reilly's teaching system: "The Lullaby seems the easiest, yet it is the most difficult movement in the entire suite — only when the performer can play legato, can they move the audience. I have been a judge in various harmonica competitions, almost all contestants can perform highly difficult showpieces, but almost no one can truly play legato."

The core of legato playing is "continuous airflow": the switch between blowing and drawing is completed instantaneously in the throat, without any interruption or change in intensity. He set a near-impossible standard: "The ultimate effect should be that no one can tell when you are blowing and when you are drawing. This is the most difficult challenge in harmonica playing."

Supplementary training includes: sustaining each note for its full value ("every note matters"); thinking in musical phrases ("no musician thinks note by note"); humming phrases to grasp the tension flow; breathing only between phrases, as breathing in the middle of a phrase "destroys the musical arc"; and "key editing" – since both C and F on the score have two different fingerings, the first task in class is to mark the fingering for each C and F on the sheet music, choosing the solution that best expresses the phrase, "you should do this for every piece you play in the future."

Singing and Staccato: Elegy and Caprice

Threnody

The lesson on Threnody emphasizes "singing": "The title of Threnody is a lament, and its core is 'singing'. You need to sing the sorrowful melody on the harmonica as if it were an operatic aria." In terms of phrasing, the C on the third beat of the first measure is a lead-up to the climax of the next phrase, "keep the volume steady, don't let the sound fade; take a small breath pause before the response."

The Caprice lesson systematizes articulation techniques: staccato "is almost like coughing, with the air being forced into the holes from the throat," rather than the tongue hitting the reed plates; hard and sharp staccato versus soft and broad staccato (almost like portamento) requires slow practice to feel the difference; the connection between staccato and legato, the contrast between strong and weak must be clearly audible; the note values in fast passages must be even — "better slow, but accurate."

Serenade: The Balance of Double Tones

"Serenade" is Reilly's own composition and also the best teaching material for practicing double-stops. This piece is played at a slow tempo (Moderato), "The most important thing is whether one can achieve a perfect legato" — he once lamented that at a certain World Harmonica Festival, "hardly any contestants could play it correctly."

Key points of double-note teaching: The thirds and sixths double-notes must be completely balanced and blended, "like a single sound"; sixths double-notes involve four holes, octaves double-notes involve five holes, with the tongue position controlling the blocked middle holes; use throat staccato to articulate notes on a continuous airflow stream, cultivating the control of "uninterrupted airflow, separated notes"; start with single-note long tones, first practicing sustained airflow, then adding double-notes.

The Rhythm of Dance and the Octave Leap

The core of the three lessons on Country Dance is "groove": the tempo must be consistent between strong and weak sections, "regardless of dynamics, the groove and tempo must remain stable"; the dynamic range of the harmonica is not as wide as other wind instruments, "special attention must always be paid, consciously utilizing the full dynamic range of the harmonica"; for octave jumps, one must find the feeling of "a single continuous motion," practicing until absolutely certain of hitting the target.

For octave leaps, Reilly designated Exercise 38 from the Green Book as mandatory—this exercise, transposable to all keys, is specifically designed to cultivate a "sense of octave distance": when moving to the octave note, keep the wrist and arm relaxed; combine the "large movement to the next octave" with the "small movement to the second," sustaining each for their full value; "every exercise should also be played musically"; after internalizing the movement, encountering octave intervals in new pieces will trigger an automatic response.

Green Book Exercise 38: Octave Jump Exercise Across All Keys

The "Russian Dance" represents the pinnacle of technical difficulty in the piece, taking five lessons to perfect: triple tongueless notes in thirds and drum-like precise accents; "if the muscles tense up, smooth movement becomes impossible" in rapid scales; glissandos achieved through oral cavity shape and breath angle; for forte passages, prioritize loudness over beautiful tone initially, "try to end this section as loudly as possible"; the Andante con moto section requires clear dynamic layers; entering the piu mosso coda, movements become smaller and more economical, with head and body remaining completely still. The method runs through all five lessons: isolate difficult passages for practice, ensure evenness and precision at extremely slow speeds before increasing tempo, never make mistakes during practice.

Nine Teaching Methods

Throughout the seventeenth lesson, Reilly's teaching method can be summarized as: keep the head stable, move the instrument; hold the harmonica as if wearing lambskin gloves; legato is the soul of the harmonica, blowing and drawing must be indistinguishable; every note matters; think in musical phrases, humming is the best way to understand phrases; slow practice is the only safe way to increase speed ("better slow and accurate than fast and sloppy"); never practice without focus; listen to your own playing; technique always serves the music — "first and foremost, you are a musician, and only then a harmonica player."

Tommy Reilly's life was not only that of a performer but also that of a composer. He composed a vast amount of original works for the chromatic harmonica, ranging from etudes and concert pieces to large-scale concertos. 9.1 Perfect Blend of Technique and Music: Valsentino (Fast Waltz)

"Fast Waltz (Valsentino)" is Tommy Reilly's most representative original work, hailed as the "touchstone of classical chromatic harmonica" and one of the most frequently chosen pieces in global harmonica competitions. Composed in the 1950s, it was a virtuosic piece created by Tommy Reilly for his own concerts, perfectly blending his technical innovations with musical ideas, and has become a benchmark for classical harmonica works. In recent years, it has also been gradually adopted by many harmonica players in China as a "beginner" practice piece for learning to play, although mastering this piece is by no means easy. On the Bilibili website, there are numerous performance videos by harmonica enthusiasts, but very few can meet the standards set by Tommy Reilly.

Creative Background

The 1950s were a crucial period for Tommy Reilly as he made a strong push into the classical music scene. At that time, many in the classical music world still believed that the harmonica could only play slow, simple melodies and was incapable of handling fast-paced, technically demanding showpieces. To break this prejudice, Tommy Reilly composed the piece Fast Waltz, using a single work to fully demonstrate the technical possibilities and musical expressiveness of the chromatic harmonica.

Musical Structure and Technical Characteristics

This piece is a typical ternary form, using a 3/8 waltz rhythm, with an overall very fast tempo, extremely high difficulty in performance, while also possessing strong musicality and dance feel, perfectly achieving "technique serving the music."

The exposition of the piece opens with a lively and agile main theme. This theme, featuring a continuous stream of sixteenth-note scales, perfectly showcases the rapid scale playing system developed by Tommy Reilly, demanding the utmost precision in keying, stability of breath, and seamless transition between blowing and drawing. At the same time, this main theme retains the dance feel of a waltz, with each musical phrase having a clear breath and rise and fall, far from being a mere display of technical prowess.

In the middle section of the piece, the melody shifts to a lyrical style, creating a stark contrast with the technical display of the exposition. This lyrical melody makes perfect use of Tommy Reilly's unique vibrato and slide techniques, with the melody line flowing continuously, as if sung by a human voice, full of emotion and depth, fully showcasing the tonal charm and expressive power of the harmonica. In this section, Tommy Reilly deliberately incorporated numerous dynamic contrasts and timbral variations, allowing performers to fully demonstrate their musical interpretation skills and enriching the musical layers of the work.

In the recapitulation section of the piece, the main theme returns, interwoven with more complex variations and cadenzas, played at a faster tempo and with heightened technical difficulty, propelling the overall mood of the work towards a climax. Finally, the piece concludes with a brilliant ascending scale, ending cleanly and leaving a lingering aftertaste.

Artistic Value

The artistic value of the Rapid Waltz lies in its pioneering demonstration to the classical music world that harmonica works can possess both extreme technical difficulty and high musical aesthetics. It is not about showing off technique for its own sake, but rather using technique to serve the musical core of the waltz, giving the piece both the impact of technical display and the lyrical beauty of music. To this day, this piece remains a core work for measuring a chromatic harmonica player's technical level and musical literacy, and Tommy Reilly's own performance of it is regarded as an unsurpassable classic model.

Analysis of Difficult Performance Techniques

"Valsentino" became a technical benchmark because it almost integrates all the core technologies of Tommy Reilly's revolutionary playing system:

Tonguing and Articulation: This piece requires the performer to master and skillfully employ hard tonguing. Hard tonguing involves concentrated force from the throat, producing short, powerful, and clear note attacks like bullets, rather than relying on the tongue harshly striking the harmonica's holes. This type of tonguing is extensively used in the theme's leaping notes, shaping the notes' elasticity, crispness, and the characteristic dance accents of a waltz.

Harmonica Technique: The precise movement of large intervals is one of the core challenges of this piece. The core principle of the Reilly system is "keep the head absolutely stable, move the harmonica only through wrist movement." In the cadenza section of Valsentino, frequent octave and even two-octave jumps must be executed by sliding the harmonica with the wrist. When playing, the lips must always stay in contact with the harmonica's comb, and at the moment the note sounds, the wrist slides the harmonica in a straight line, along the shortest path to the target hole, ensuring that the airflow is not interrupted during the jump.

Fast Chromatic Scale: Reilly emphasizes the necessity of applying the "pre-action of the slide" technique. This means that while moving the harmonica, the finger responsible for pressing the slide button must anticipate the action, ensuring that at the moment the note sounds, the button is already in the fully pressed or fully released correct state. This requires breath, wrist movement, and finger button pressing to achieve millisecond-level precise synchronization.

Double Stops and Chords: The works also involve the legato playing of double stops and chords. For example, in certain parts of lyrical and cadenza passages, it is required to use the tongue-blocking technique to simultaneously play thirds or sixths intervals, maintaining perfect balance and volume blending between the two notes, while achieving seamless legato between blow and draw transitions.

9.2 Romantic Poetic Expression: Serenade

"Serenade" is the most romantic concert piece among Tommy Reilly's original compositions and one of his most frequently performed and beloved original works by audiences. This piece perfectly showcases Tommy Reilly's exquisite control of tone and his profound understanding of Romantic music, making it a quintessential example of a classical harmonica lyrical work.

Creative Background

This piece was composed in the late 1950s — German harmonica player Uwe Warschkow recalls that during his studies in Trossingen from 1956-1959, he heard Reilly himself playing this serenade outside a local hotel. By this time, Tommy Reilly had already established himself in the classical music world and no longer needed to prove himself with showy pieces; his creative focus began to shift towards emotional expression and poetic content in music.

Musical Structure and Artistic Features

This piece is in ternary form, played at a moderate tempo (Moderato) — verified according to the TRM series sheet music published by franchbach, dispelling the commonly circulated "Andante" claim. The melody is graceful and lyrical, imbued with the poetry and tenderness of Romanticism, perfectly showcasing the vocal quality and tonal charm of the harmonica.

The A section of the piece opens with a soothing, lyrical main melody. This melody features a stepwise melodic progression, with phrases that flow seamlessly, much like natural and fluid vocal singing. In this melody, Tommy Reilly deliberately incorporates numerous breath swells and vibrato techniques, allowing every note of the melody to possess a rich emotional fluctuation. This fully showcases his signature "silver sound" tone, which is warm, transparent, and filled with deep emotion.

In the B section of the piece, the melody shifts to a different key, and the mood becomes fuller and more intense, forming a stark contrast with the gentleness of the A section. This melody, with a higher register and stronger emotional tension, showcases Tommy Reilly's exceptional breath control, achieving extreme dynamic contrasts. The harmonica's tone ranges from a soft whisper to a rich and full sound, demonstrating a remarkable dynamic range and expressive power. Additionally, this section incorporates skillful slides and grace notes, enhancing the melody's lyrical quality and deepening the poetic essence of the work.

In the recapitulation section, the main theme returns. After the emotional buildup of the B section, the melody of the recapitulation gains an additional layer of deeper sentiment. Ultimately, the melody gradually descends, concluding with a very soft long note, leaving a lingering aftertaste. It is like the ending of a love poem, tender and moving.

Artistic Value

This "Serenade" completely shattered the stereotype that "the harmonica cannot express the delicate lyricism of classical works." Tommy Reilly, with this piece, proved that the harmonica's tone possesses an emotional expressiveness that rivals the violin and the human voice. It can perfectly interpret the poetry and deep affection of Romantic music, capturing the heart of every listener in a concert hall with just a small instrument. To this day, this piece remains the most frequently performed lyrical work at harmonica concerts worldwide, and Tommy Reilly's own recording of it is revered by countless music fans as "the sound of healing for the soul."

9.4 The Artistic Value of Lesser-Known Classics: Other Original Pieces

In addition to the aforementioned classics, Tommy Reilly also composed a large number of original concert miniatures. These pieces, though not lengthy, each possess unique characteristics and fully showcase his compositional talent and musical vision. Among the most representative are La Ronde De L'Amour (The Waltz of Love), Firefly, The Night Of The Fourth, and others.

"La Ronde De L'Amour (The Waltz of Love)" is a waltz piece brimming with French romanticism, featuring a lively and graceful melody imbued with tender affection. This work blends the melodic characteristics of French chanson with classical music composition techniques, offering a catchy tune while maintaining a high artistic quality, perfectly showcasing Tommy Reilly's mastery of diverse musical styles.

"Firefly" is a highly evocative piece for piano and harmonica. The composition employs rapid arpeggiated melodies to mimic the lively dance of fireflies, with a melody that is light and agile, and a tone that is clear and bright, like the flickering fireflies in the night sky, full of childlike wonder and poetry. This piece demands an extremely high level of breath control and soft playing ability from the performer, with each note needing to be as delicate, translucent, and full of life as the glow of a firefly.

"The Night Of The Fourth" is a film score composed by Tommy Reilly, and it is one of his rare original works with a suspenseful character. This piece employs extensive chromatic scales and dissonant chords, creating a tense and suspenseful atmosphere through the interplay of bright and dark timbres and rhythmic tension. It fully demonstrates the unlimited potential of the harmonica in dramatic musical expression and breaks the stereotype that "the harmonica can only play lyrical and cheerful melodies."

9.5 Nationalist School and Folk Music Arrangements: British Folk Song Series

Tommy Reilly's arrangements were not limited to the works of classical music masters but also included a vast number of British folk songs and European folk music pieces. He always believed that folk music was the root of the harmonica, and that the harmonica, as an instrument, had a natural affinity with folk music. His arranged series of British folk songs became a classic example of the classical adaptation of folk music.

One of the most representative is his rearranged work in the album English Folk Songs/Harmonica and Harp with harpist Skaila Kanga. In this album, he arranged dozens of traditional English folk songs, including classics like "Greensleeves" and "Scarborough Fair". In his arrangements, he did not simply play the folk melodies, but used classical music composition techniques to re-orchestrate the pieces, giving the simple folk melodies richer harmonic layers and more complete musical structures, while retaining the simplicity and purity of folk music.

In his performances, he used the harmonica's warm tone to perfectly interpret the gentleness and poetry of British folk songs, blending seamlessly with the ethereal sound of the harp, giving these folk songs that have been passed down for hundreds of years a new artistic vitality. Upon its release, this album became a classic of British folk song arrangements, repeatedly played by BBC radio, and also made more ordinary listeners fall in love with the harmonica through these familiar folk melodies.

9.6 Exploration and Adaptation of Crossover Works: Jazz and Latin Style Works

Tommy Reilly's vision for adaptation was never limited to classical and folk music. Throughout his life, he explored the infinite possibilities of the harmonica, arranging a vast number of jazz and Latin-style pieces. These adaptations not only showcased his diverse musical tastes but also propelled the development of the harmonica across various musical genres.

In his jazz arrangements, he adapted classics by jazz masters such as Gershwin and Duke Ellington. Unlike traditional blues harmonica playing, his jazz arrangements perfectly blend the rigorous techniques of classical music with the improvisational style of jazz, preserving the freedom and agility of jazz while possessing the refinement and precision of classical music, thus creating a unique classical jazz harmonica style. His arrangement of Gershwin's "Rhapsody in Blue" excerpt has become a classic model for jazz harmonica performance.

In his Latin-style arrangements, his adaptation of El Cumbanchero is one of the most beloved pieces among harmonica enthusiasts worldwide. This piece, with its strong rhythm and lively melody, is filled with the passion and vitality of Latin music. Tommy Reilly's arrangement perfectly retains the original Latin rhythm and melodic characteristics, while adding challenging fast scales and virtuosic passages, allowing the harmonica performance to blend seamlessly with the passion of Latin music, making it highly captivating on stage. To this day, this arrangement remains one of the most energizing pieces at global harmonica concerts.

From The Vagabond to 55 Days at Peking: The Chromatic Harmonica as the Preferred Choice of Hollywood's Scoring Maestros

In 1960, Reilly recorded the theme music for the film The Sundowners, directed by Fred Zinnemann - Dimitri Tiomkin's Theme from "The Sundowners" and Down Under, accompanied by Wally Stott's orchestra (Philips PN 1094 single; reissued in 1961 as the Harmonica Magic EP, featuring tracks like Yokohama Holiday). The film's depiction of an Australian outback grazing family naturally aligned with the harmonica's "folk, rustic sentiment."

In 1963, he recorded the two soundtrack pieces So Little Time and Moon Fire for the epic film 55 Days at Peking directed by Nicholas Ray (music by Tiomkin, Philips PB 1248, in collaboration with the Ivor Raymonde Choir and Orchestra). In the same year, he also recorded for German Western films (Dakota, S.O.S., Oriole CB 1833, with The Tradesmen as accompaniment).

In the 1970s, his film score work extended to Scandinavia: in 1976, the Norwegian animated feature Flåklypa Grand Prix (also known as Pinchcliffe Grand Prix) had music by Bent Fabricius-Bjerre, with all harmonica parts played by Reilly (Polydor 2382 066 soundtrack album); in 1979, the British film Tarka the Otter featured music by David Fanshawe, with Reilly performing alongside an instrumental ensemble (Argo ZSW 613).

In 1981, he recorded the album Winnetou Melodien (Teldec 6.24965 AS) for the famous German Winnetou western film series: Old Shatterhand-Melodie, Apanatschi, Winnetou Melodie, Grand Canyon-Melodie, and Chinla-River-Song, all accompanied by the Munich Radio Orchestra conducted by Martin Böttcher himself – Böttcher being the composer of the film scores for the Winnetou series.

In 1994, the posthumous score of Bernard Herrmann's The Night Digger was reissued on CD by X label (LXCD 12), featuring Reilly's harmonica playing in Scenario Macabre for Orchestra, conducted by Herrmann himself with the Sessions of London Orchestra. In the same year, he also made a guest appearance on harmonica in Canadian composer André Gagnon's album Romantique (Star STR-CD-8057), playing in the track Le pianiste envolé, conducted by John Coleman with the National Philharmonic Orchestra.

The Golden Age of British Television: Harmonica Themes

Reilly left behind many memorable theme songs for British TV and radio comedies:

  • 1953 Theme from The Night of the Fourth (Parlophone R 3693, conducted by Eric Spear) — Note: This piece was not composed by Reilly, but by May, and is often mistakenly attributed to Reilly.

  • 1954 Theme song for the BBC family drama The Grove Family, "Family Joke" (composed by Eric Spear, Parlophone R 3860). This piece was later widely used as production music.

  • 1958 Theme song for the ITV police drama Dixon of Dock Green, "An Ordinary Copper" (Oriole CB 1426, featuring actor Jack Warner). This is one of the most famous harmonica themes in British television history.

  • 1960 Theme music for the BBC radio comedy The Navy Lark (Hohner T 4007). Reilly co-created this melody with James Moody, which later became a classic; the track Trade Wind Hornpipe from the music album is an instrumental arrangement of the theme music. In 1996, EMI Premier's album Vintage Themes from British Radio, Television & the Newsreels included the original recordings of these two themes.

Building a Music Library: The Vast Repertoire Behind the Pseudonym

In addition to commercial recordings, Reilly also recorded hundreds of tracks for production music libraries—these short, functional pieces of music were used as background music in films, television, and radio. He collaborated with James Moody and his son David Reilly, using various pseudonyms—Max Martin, T Rundle, Dave Holland, Dwight Barker—to record a vast amount of music for companies such as Conroy, Berry Music, Chappell, Weinberger (JW Media Music), AudioBMP, and Boosey and Hawkes.

The Conroy company's music album Harmonica (KCR0160, 29 minutes, 49 tracks) is a collection of works co-owned 50% by Reilly and Moody, featuring tracks such as Sophisticated Party, Wind In Her Hair, Trade Wind Hornpipe, Child's Play, Hurry Hurry, Pins And Needles, Night Out, Shooting The Rapids, Alley Cat, Mirabelle, and more—each only lasting from a few dozen seconds to a minute, representing the pinnacle of "functional music."

It is noteworthy that Reilly's recordings credited as "T. Rundle" or "Rundle-Morris" (shared with Moody) (such as Esmeralda, Tokyo, No Time) are an extension of this pseudonym system - "Rundle" being derived from his real name Thomas Rundle Reilly.

Listening Methodology: Listening Like Reilly

Uwe Warschkow notes that Reilly required his students to "listen critically to themselves," and the same approach applies to appreciating his recordings:

  1. Listen to the musical phrases first, then the technique: Pay attention to how he handles the phrasing, breathing, and climax of the music, rather than focusing on speed first.

  1. Appreciate His Tone: Tommy Reilly's tone is warm and expressive, which is a hallmark of his signature style.

  2. Distinguishing Blow and Draw: He pursued throughout his life the goal that "listeners can never tell which note is blown and which is drawn" — when listening to his long phrases, try to find the breath points, and you will find that they are almost undetectable.

  3. Comparing the Same Piece Across Different Eras: His vibrato evolved from the early "more intense, more violin-like" style to a later "more restrained, purer" approach (as observed by Zheng Zhangming).

  4. Listen with the sheet music: The best way to learn his interpretation is to listen to the recordings while following the sheet music for Valsentino, Serenade, and Toledo (Franchbach engraved edition) included with this book.

12.3 Honors and Later Life

Tommy Reilly was awarded the MBE (Member of the Most Excellent Order of the British Empire) in 1992, personally bestowed by Queen Elizabeth II, for "services to music." He remains the only harmonica player to have received a British royal honor. American author Kim Fields, in his harmonica reference book, states: "Among that small elite group to which he belongs, Tommy Reilly is generally regarded as the finest classical performer."

Igor Stravinsky once said, "After hearing you play my Russian Song, I would be delighted for you to perform any of my works."

Reilly's stage persona - typically dressed in a white tuxedo while performing - became his signature, earning him the moniker "The Man in White" from critics. This image held multiple layers of meaning: visually, the white color created a striking focal point under stage lights, reinforcing the soloist's central position; symbolically, white suggested purity, refinement, and a sense of the ethereal, contrasting with the harmonica's "folk" origins and elevating the instrument's cultural status.

Entering the 1980s, Reilly's public performance activities gradually decreased, but he still maintained selective performances, especially at music festivals and special occasions. His last professional performance was in 1998 at the Dartington International Summer Music School in Devon, where he conducted a masterclass for students from four continents. This choice was symbolic: he focused his later years on educational legacy rather than personal performances, reflecting his deep concern for the long-term development of the harmonica art.

In December 1993, Reilly suffered a severe car accident and sustained serious injuries, after which his health deteriorated. Uwe Warschkow recalled that even after that, he still insisted that students come to Hammonds Wood for lessons, showing no reduction in his passion and intensity for teaching.

In his later years, Reilly devoted more energy to teaching and writing. He continued to accept selected students for individual instruction, especially during the summer performance breaks; he held workshops at harmonica festivals, influencing numerous players; his textbooks and etudes became standard teaching literature. These efforts ensured the systematic transmission of his technical system and artistic philosophy, extending his influence beyond the limitations of his personal performance career.

12.4 The Last Masterclass

In 1997, Chen Guanbin stumbled upon an exciting piece of news on the renowned harmonica forum Harp-L: the following year (1998), Tommy Reilly and Sigmund Groven were going to jointly host a harmonica masterclass at the Dartington Music Summer School in the UK.

Dartington Music Summer School is a renowned music summer course in the UK, located at Dartington Hall in Devon, boasting a long history and beautiful surroundings. It is not only a place for learning but also an important platform for musicians to exchange ideas.

Upon learning this news, Chen Guanbin spent all his savings and traveled across the ocean just to attend this masterclass. What he did not expect was that this journey would become the most important experience of his harmonica career, and it would also be Tommy Reilly's last public teaching activity.

This is clearly documented in history - the 1998 Dartington was Reilly's last known public teaching appearance.

Sigmund Groven was one of Norway's most important chromatic harmonica masters, then in his prime, enjoying a lofty status in the Nordic classical harmonica community. His joint teaching with Tommy Reilly represented the confluence of the British and Nordic schools.

Groven later recalled this collaboration with Reilly, also mentioning Reilly's strict demands for musical expression. Groven himself was a performer who pursued perfect tone quality, and his exchange with Reilly at this music camp became an important chapter in the exchange between the Nordic and British harmonica schools. The two masters' teaching styles might have differed: Reilly represented the traditional, strictly classical school influenced by violin training; Groven, on the other hand, might have brought more of the unique freshness and ethnic colors of Nordic music. For the participating students, being able to receive guidance from both masters at the same time was a rare opportunity.

At the 1998 Dartington Music Camp, Chen Guanbin also met another important figure - James Hughes.

James Hughes, known as the "Father of the Modern World Harmonica Festival," is the visionary behind the current scale of the World Harmonica Festival. He hurriedly arrived at the music camp after learning about it at the last minute, bringing with him a set of "Essential Exercises for the Chromatic Harmonica" (2 volumes) that he had just completed.

In 1998, the internet was not yet widespread, and Chen Guanbin had no idea how famous James Hughes was. He got to know Hughes because he accidentally learned that Hughes was the agent for James Moody's harmonica sheet music and was writing practice pieces for him.

This chance encounter allowed Chen Guanbin to obtain the precious teaching materials written by Hughes, and also informed him about the organizational structure and historical development of the World Harmonica Championships.

"Never ever play the hole 4 C and Hole 8 C"

At some point at Dartington, when Chen Guanbin was chatting with James Hughes, Hughes suddenly said to him with a serious expression a highly technical sentence:

"Never ever play the hole 4 C and Hole 8 C"

(Absolutely absolutely do not blow the 4th hole C and the 8th hole C)

Chén Guānbīn was full of questions at the time. Although he knew it might be related to the structure of the harmonica and the uniformity of its tone, Hughes's firm statement left a deep impression on him. After the music camp ended, Chén Guānbīn went to Douglas Tate's house for a few more days of lessons. In the class, Chén Guānbīn tentatively asked, "Should this C be played on the 4th hole or the 5th hole?"

Unexpectedly, Tate also said the same thing unequivocally:

"Never ever play the hole 4 C and Hole 8 C"

At that moment, Chen Guanbin had a sudden realization - there was indeed this third technique. From then on, he had no more doubts or hesitations, directly skipping the 4th hole and the 8th hole C, and never again faced any difficulty in choosing.

This principle is based on the physical characteristics of the harmonica's construction: the C notes (draw notes) on holes 4 and 8 differ in timbre and volume from other holes. On a chromatic harmonica, these holes often produce a timbre that is less uniform compared to other holes due to differences in reed size, chamber size, and aerodynamic properties. For classical players who strive for tonal consistency, avoiding these "problem holes" and using alternative fingerings (such as using the C draw on hole 5 instead of the C draw on hole 4) is one of the key techniques for achieving a professional-level sound.

This principle reflects the refinement of classical harmonica playing - not only hitting the correct pitches but also ensuring that each note's timbre, volume, and quality are perfectly unified.

12.5 An Eternal Musical Legacy

Tommy Reilly passed away on September 25, 2000, at his home in Frensham, Surrey, England, at the age of 81. He was laid to rest in the village churchyard of Frensham, with a simple yet powerful epitaph on his gravestone: "He was the greatest". This assessment, coming from his peers, students, and the wider music community, stands as the ultimate confirmation of his artistic achievements. His granddaughter, Georgina Reilly, later became a Canadian film and television actress, continuing the family's artistic tradition.

Memorial Concerts and Performance Events

After Tommy Reilly's passing, the global harmonica community immediately organized numerous memorial concerts, with top harmonica players from around the world paying tribute to this harmonica pioneer through their performances.

The most representative event was the Tommy Reilly Memorial Concert held at the World Harmonica Festival in Trossingen, Germany in 2025. This concert was the most significant event in the global harmonica community in 2025, bringing together top harmonica players such as Sigmund Groven and Yasuo Watani from around the world to fully perform Tommy Reilly's original and arranged works, paying tribute to the father of the chromatic harmonica with a performance that transcended time and space. The concert hall was packed, and thousands of harmonica enthusiasts from around the globe listened to the performance together. When the melody of Fast Waltz began, the audience erupted into thunderous applause that lasted for a long time.

In addition to this commemorative concert, dozens of Tommy Reilly tribute concerts are held annually around the world, from London, England, to Oslo, Norway, from Tokyo, Japan, to Shanghai, China. His compositions are always the centerpiece of harmonica concerts, and his melodies continue to resonate in concert halls across the globe.

International Competitions and Awards

In memory of Tommy Reilly and to promote the growth of young harmonica players, several international harmonica competitions around the world have established the Tommy Reilly Memorial Category and special awards.

Among the most influential is the Tommy Reilly Classical Harmonica category at the World Harmonica Championships in Trossingen, Germany. This category is one of the most prestigious at the World Harmonica Championships, requiring contestants to perform Tommy Reilly's original compositions to assess their classical harmonica playing skills and musical interpretation abilities. This category has become the ultimate arena for young classical harmonica players worldwide, with countless young musicians embarking on their professional playing careers through this category.

Meanwhile, international harmonica competitions in countries such as Norway, the UK, and Japan have established the Tommy Reilly Special Award to recognize outstanding young performers in the field of classical harmonica playing. These competitions and awards not only serve as a tribute to Tommy Reilly but also as a continuation of his artistic philosophy, promoting the continuous development of classical harmonica art.

Academic Research and Teaching Legacy

As the art of the harmonica continues to develop, the global musicology community's academic research on Tommy Reilly's performance art, teaching philosophy, and compositions is becoming increasingly in-depth.

Scholars and graduate students from numerous music institutions around the world have written extensive academic papers and monographs on Tommy Reilly, conducting in-depth research from various dimensions including his life experiences, technical innovations, performance philosophy, compositions, and teaching methodologies. His works are listed as required repertoire for harmonica majors, his performance system is established as the core content of harmonica education, and his artistic concepts have become the central theme of harmonica art academic research.

Meanwhile, multiple music institutions around the world have established Tommy Reilly harmonica teaching programs and research centers, systematically organizing his teaching materials, audio and video recordings, and manuscripts, inheriting his teaching system and artistic philosophy, and promoting academic research and educational development of the harmonica art.

Tommy Reilly passed away in 2000, but his musical legacy endures. His recordings continue to be listened to and studied by harmonica enthusiasts around the world, his instructional materials continue to guide a new generation of harmonica learners, and the pieces he commissioned continue to be performed in concerts.

Reilly's story is one of dreams, perseverance, and achievement. Starting from a small city in Canada, he endured the hardships of war and ultimately became a world-renowned harmonica player. He proved that with talent, perseverance, and belief, any instrument can find a place in the halls of classical music.

Reilly's legacy continues to manifest in contemporary harmonica art: the mainstreaming of classical harmonica playing—from harmonica entering conservatory curricula to the normalization of international competitions; the normalization of crossover performances—from the breakdown of barriers between the serious and the popular to the widespread acceptance of style fusion; the maturation of international exchange networks—from the globalization of harmonica festivals to the rise of online teaching platforms. These trends can all be traced back to Reilly's pioneering work from half a century ago.

12.6 A Spiritual Icon in the Hearts of Global Harmonica Players

Today, more than 20 years have passed since Tommy Reilly's passing, and more than 80 years have passed since he initiated the harmonica technique revolution in the prisoner-of-war camp. However, his artistic philosophy, performance system, and teaching ideology remain the core guidance for the development of contemporary harmonica art, and he is still the spiritual idol in the hearts of harmonica players worldwide.

The development of contemporary harmonica art, whether in the deep exploration of the classical field or in the exploration of crossover genres, has always followed the path paved by Tommy Reilly. In the realm of classical harmonica, today's top harmonica players still adhere to his core principle of "technique serving music," striving to enhance the artistic expression of the harmonica and promoting more composers to write works for the harmonica, thereby giving the harmonica a more important position in the classical music world. In the crossover field, contemporary harmonica players are exploring the infinite possibilities of the harmonica in various genres such as jazz, pop, rock, and film scores, continuing his open artistic vision and making the harmonica a beloved instrument for more people.

At the same time, with the advancement of technology, the design and manufacture of harmonicas continue to follow Tommy Reilly's philosophy - constantly optimizing the instrument's performance around the needs of the player, giving the harmonica stronger expressiveness, more stable quality, and richer tonal possibilities. Whether it's traditional harmonica manufacturers or emerging instrument design brands, they still refer to the design concepts he proposed, driving continuous innovation in the harmonica manufacturing industry.

More importantly, the spirit of Tommy Reilly continues to inspire harmonica enthusiasts around the world. He spent his entire life fighting against societal prejudice, creating possibilities where there seemed none, and elevating an unrecognized instrument into the halls of classical music. This passion for music, dedication to ideals, and pursuit of innovation are not only the spiritual wealth of harmonica art but also the spiritual benchmark for all musicians.

13.1 The Era of the Three Greats: Adler, Sebastian, and Reilly

In the mid-20th century, the classical/chromatic harmonica world was dominated by three giants:

Larry Adler (1914-2001) - Legendary American-born performer. Adler brought the harmonica into Hollywood, jazz, and classical concert halls, collaborating with composers like George Gershwin, Duke Ellington, Ralph Vaughan Williams, Darius Milhaud, and William Walton. He commissioned and premiered works such as Vaughan Williams' Romance, Milhaud's Suite, and Benjamin's Harmonica Concerto (premiered at the 1953 Royal Albert Hall Proms). His style was extroverted, dramatic, and pop-oriented; his vibrato was primarily tongue vibrato. Reilly's relationship with Adler was one of "mutual respect as colleagues": Adler described Reilly as "unique, in a class of his own... he doesn't even have a close competitor." The concertos by Villa-Lobos and Cowell were originally commissioned for Adler, and Reilly later became an important interpreter of these works.

John Sebastian Sr. (1914-1980) - American classical harmonica player and composer, another performer besides Adler who commissioned numerous works (Villa-Lobos' 1955 concerto, Henry Cowell's 1962 concerto were all commissioned by him). He established the academic path of "harmonica education" and taught at the Manhattan School of Music in his later years. Sebastian's concert repertoire often included Bach's polyphonic arrangements - which is similar to Reilly's approach of adapting Bach through the Studies.

Tommy Reilly (1919-2000) -- The Latecomer Who Perfected the Art. Among the three greats, he was the only one who accomplished four things: building a complete technical system (violin-like harmonica handling/legato/tonal theory), establishing a large-scale commissioning network (over 30 concerto-level works), achieving an instrument revolution (chromatic harmonica), and creating an international teaching system (TRIC+ teaching materials). Adler had greater public recognition, Sebastian had earlier academic influence, but Reilly's "systematic" approach was unparalleled.

13.2 Jazz and Pop Routes: Toots and the Harmonicats

Toots Thielemans (1922-2016) - Belgian jazz harmonica master who brought the chromatic harmonica into the heart of jazz improvisation. He collaborated extensively with Quincy Jones, Bill Evans, and Ella Fitzgerald. The harmonica in the film Midnight Cowboy was played by him (the soundtrack album was recorded by Reilly, see the film score chapter in this book). Reilly and Toots represented two directions in harmonica art: classical tradition vs. jazz improvisation; the two respected each other and jointly elevated the status of the harmonica as an instrument.

Jerry Murad and the Harmonicats (The trio that sold a million copies with 1947's "Peg O' My Heart") - The "King of the Harmonica" who brought the harmonica trio into the pop music scene. Reilly and Murad were good friends, and Chapter 7 of the manuscript records the anecdote of Reilly perfectly imitating Murad's tone, causing Murad to jokingly curse "You bastard." Murad himself was also a member of TRIC.

13.3 The European School: From Germany to Scandinavia

Trossingen, Germany (Hohner headquarters) is the European harmonica stronghold: Willi Burger (1955 World Champion), Franz Chmel (1963 World Champion, Austrian classical harmonica master, whose complete recordings were systematically collected by the author of this book), Helmuth Herold and Bernhard Schwarz (Uwe Warschkow's German teacher) constituted the post-war German school; Uwe Warschkow himself won first place in the chamber music category at the World Harmonica Festival in 1993. The relationship between the German school and Reilly is "coexistence of competition and exchange" -- it was in Karlsruhe in 1967 that Douglas Tate defeated competitors from various countries to become the first British World Champion.

The Norwegian school directly originated from Reilly: Groven became Reilly's most orthodox heir and, after the 1970s, the flag bearer of Nordic harmonica (soloist with the Salzburg Mozart Orchestra, performances for the Norwegian royal family, commissioned new concertos by Sommerro and Kapstad); Georg Pollestad's Polle workshop represented the Nordic approach to "instrument making" – Reilly himself called the Polle harmonica the "Rolls-Royce of all harmonicas" in his later years.

13.4 East: The Legacy in Japan and China

Yasuo Watani (1960-) - Japan's foremost classical harmonica player and a direct disciple of Reilly. After studying in the UK, he returned to Japan and became a long-time professor at Senzoku Gakuen College of Music, integrating Reilly's teaching system into Japanese music education; he has served as a judge for the World Harmonica Festival in Trossingen multiple times; in his later years, he performed with Douglas Tate in Singapore (2004) and participated in the 2025 Trossingen Reilly Memorial Concert. There is another unique vessel for Reilly's legacy in the Japanese harmonica community: the Japanese edition of Studies for Chromatic Harmonica revised and demonstrated by Kenji Sakimoto - this is currently the only printed version still available for purchase.

Tommy Reilly and the Chinese Harmonica Community: The manuscript author, along with Chen Guanbin and Cheng Jang Ming, have presented Reilly's reception in the Chinese-speaking world: Chen Guanbin traveled to Dartington in 1998 to attend Reilly's last masterclass; Cheng Jang Ming wrote about TRIC in the magazine Harmonica World; pieces like Toledo have been adopted by domestic harmonica teaching institutions as advanced compulsory repertoire, and major international competitions (Hong Kong International Harmonica Festival, World Harmonica Championships) also frequently feature them as selected pieces.
